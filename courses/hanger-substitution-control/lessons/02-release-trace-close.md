# Releasing, Tracing and Closing Approvals
Once the tests are done, the substitute needs a decision that people can act on. This lesson covers the last two questions of the exception record, which cartons contain the substitute and when the approval ends, and shows how to put your decision where packers, inspectors and receivers will use it.

## Approve a defined release
A good approval has clear limits. Consider this hypothetical example from the article:
- The order is for 12,000 garments across three styles.
- The substitute passes checks on two trouser styles but leaves a visible impression on the lightweight skirt.
- The buyer authorises up to 4,000 substitute hangers for the two trouser styles, in the first shipment only.
- The skirt keeps the original hanger or waits for a different option.

The numbers simply show the limit of that release. On a real order, replace them with the quantities and order lines on the actual purchase order. A **defined release** states the styles, quantities, destinations, approval number, packing instructions, carton range and receiving checks it covers.

## Put the decision where people work
Place the decision in the documents each team already uses:

| Where | What it should show |
|---|---|
| Order amendment | The substitute and its approval number |
| Packing instruction | Styles that may use the substitute, and styles to set aside if approved stock runs out |
| Quality control | An example or annotated image of the replacement |
| Cartons | Whether each was packed to the normal instruction or the exception |

If inspectors open several cartons, they should be able to tell which instruction each one was packed against.

## Accept compromises explicitly
Sometimes a difference is acceptable in one place but not in another. A different hook shade might be permissible on a shipment for stockroom use, yet look visibly inconsistent on coordinated front-of-store rails. The written approval can **distinguish destinations**. That gives the supplier and the warehouse a shared instruction, and leaves the final merchandising decision with the buyer.

## Make the exception visible at receiving
Once packing begins, link the exception to a **lot or carton range**, and agree how it will appear on the packing list and in the receiving instructions. Then:
1. Ask the receiver to compare the first cartons in the affected range with the approved sample or its reference photos.
2. If a carton has an unexpected component, preserve its identifier before segregating it.
3. Use that identifier to make the next check specific to the shipment and to help the factory trace which instructions were used.

GS1’s Global Traceability Standard describes recording data about events such as packing, shipping and receiving. You can apply that principle with whatever order and warehouse systems you already have. The practical test is whether the receiving team can connect the carton it opens to the component approval that applies to it. A supplier’s email saying it used the approved alternative cannot do that job on its own.

> **Key idea:** Traceability means the person opening a carton can link it to the approval that covers it, without relying on the supplier’s word.

## Close the approval when the shortage ends
Set an **end condition** at the same time as you approve the substitute. It could be a purchase-order line, a maximum quantity or a shipment cutoff. Then:
- Ask the supplier to reconcile how many substitutes it used and whether any remain.
- If the original part is available again, confirm which shipment restarts the normal specification.
- If the substitute should become the permanent version, review it for the wider assortment and issue a new revision through the usual approval process.

## Keep the record short
The exception record does not need to be long. Give it an owner and include:
- the reason for the change
- the two sample IDs, approved and substitute
- the eligible garment styles
- the receiving identifier
- the end condition

The next time a factory asks whether an available part can be used, the answer can move quickly from the sample room to the right cartons, without silently changing the specification for the next order.

## Key takeaways
- Approve a defined release that names the styles, quantities, destinations, approval number, carton range and receiving checks.
- Put the decision into the order amendment, the packing instruction and the quality control reference.
- Use destination-specific approvals to accept compromises explicitly.
- Link the exception to a lot or carton range so receivers can check the first cartons against the sample.
- Set an end condition, reconcile usage, and make a permanent change only through a new revision.
