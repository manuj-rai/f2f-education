# Testing a Proposed Substitute
A hanger substitution often starts with a short supplier message. The approved clip is out of stock, an alternative that fits the same hanger body can arrive before the packing date, and the factory wants to know whether it can go ahead. This lesson shows how to gather the right evidence and test the substitute before you say yes.

## The decision you are making
Hangers are part of an order’s packaging trims, and a small change to one part can affect how the garment looks and behaves once it is packed. When a supplier proposes a substitute, you are balancing two things: keeping the order on schedule, and preserving the way the garment hangs, grips and arrives.

The same method applies whichever part is short: a clip, a hook, a coating or the hanger body itself.

The decision becomes much easier when every temporary approval can be traced to the styles, quantities and shipments it covers. A useful **exception record** answers four questions:
1. **What changed?**
2. **Where does it work?** That is, which styles and destinations it suits.
3. **Which cartons contain it?**
4. **When does the approval end?**

This lesson deals with the first two questions. Lesson 2 covers the other two.

## Step 1: Get the facts from the supplier
Ask the supplier to identify:
- the exact component that is unavailable
- the approved version
- the proposed substitute
- the earliest delivery date for each option

Then request a **side-by-side photo** with both versions clearly labelled. Use it to spot differences that matter for this application, for example a different jaw face on a clip, a taller hook, or a shoulder shape that sits differently under a garment. If the change involves a coating, ask which surfaces will touch the fabric.

## Step 2: Agree acceptance criteria first
Agree on the **acceptance criteria** before you compare the samples, so everyone knows in advance what counts as a pass. Different questions need different kinds of evidence:

| Question | Suitable evidence |
|---|---|
| Does the garment hang level? | A photograph may be enough |
| Does the clip grip securely? | A named garment and a repeatable check |
| Does it mark the fabric? | A named garment and a repeatable check |

## Step 3: Test a complete sample on real garments
A photo shows shape, not performance. Ask for a **complete hanger** with the substitute part fitted, and put it beside the approved version. Use the **same garment** on each, so the only difference is the hanger.

Test the styles most likely to expose a problem. For a clip change, that could mean:
- a lightweight skirt that could slip through the pads
- a trouser with a thicker waistband that is harder to clamp

After removing the garments, check the fabric under the same light. Reproduce the ordinary handling and packing conditions that the factory or store will use, so the test reflects what will really happen to the garment.

> **Key idea:** Test the substitute on the garments most likely to fail, not the ones most likely to pass. A clip that holds a thick waistband may still let a light skirt slip or leave a mark on it.

## Step 4: Record and share the result
Write down the garment style tested, the sample references and what the reviewer observed. Send the same result to the people responsible for **quality, sourcing and packing**, so each team works from one shared finding.

## Key takeaways
- A useful exception record answers four questions: what changed, where it works, which cartons contain it and when it ends.
- Ask the supplier for the exact part, both versions, delivery dates and a labelled side-by-side photo.
- Agree acceptance criteria before comparing samples; grip and marking need a named garment and a repeatable check.
- Test a complete hanger with the same garment on each version, choosing the styles most likely to expose a problem.
- Record the result and share it with quality, sourcing and packing.
