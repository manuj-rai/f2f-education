# Smarter suits for longer missions
As humanity prepares for longer missions to the Moon, Mars and potentially beyond, space clothing is moving towards lighter, smarter and more multifunctional materials. In this final lesson you will look at where space textiles are heading, and how ideas developed for space connect with what people wear on Earth.

## From a spacewalk to a planetary mission
A short spacewalk and a multi-month planetary mission are completely different textile challenges. On longer missions, especially planned lunar and Mars exploration, astronauts may need clothing designed for a very different way of living and working.

This future is already taking shape through NASA's **Artemis programme**. Axiom Space is developing the **Axiom Extravehicular Mobility Unit (AxEMU)** for Artemis III. The suit is designed with improved mobility, enhanced protection and a wider range of sizing. NASA says it is intended to accommodate at least 90 per cent of the US male and female population, which reflects a growing emphasis on fit and inclusivity in spacesuit design.

## Smart textiles for astronauts
Future space textiles are likely to go beyond passive protection. **Smart textiles**, also known as **e-textiles**, integrate sensors and electronic components into fabrics. Such clothing could monitor:
- body temperature
- heart rate
- movement
- hydration and other physiological indicators

For astronauts on long-duration missions, this monitoring could become increasingly valuable. Instead of relying entirely on separate devices, sensors could be integrated directly into garments, so the clothing itself becomes part of the astronaut's health-monitoring system.

This reflects a wider change in fashion, where garments are becoming platforms for technology. Fitness trackers and smartwatches have already introduced consumers to wearable technology; future space garments could take the idea much further.

## What future garments could be
Future space garments could potentially be:

| Quality | Benefit |
|---|---|
| Lighter | Reduces the physical burden on astronauts |
| More flexible | Allows greater freedom of movement |
| Temperature-regulating | Responds to changing environmental conditions |
| Sensor-enabled | Monitors health and performance |
| Self-repairing | Reduces the need for frequent replacement |
| Antimicrobial | Helps manage hygiene in closed environments |
| Modular | Damaged parts are replaced, not the whole garment |
| Recyclable or renewable | Made from such materials where practical |

> **Key idea:** On Earth, people own different garments for different purposes. On a long space mission, carrying large quantities of clothing is impractical, so an astronaut's wardrobe must do more with less.

## Self-healing and responsive materials
One particularly exciting research area is **self-healing materials**. Scientists are exploring polymers and other materials that can repair small amounts of damage automatically or with minimal intervention. If the technology becomes reliable enough for space, a small tear that repairs itself could reduce maintenance and extend the life of protective equipment.

Researchers are also investigating advanced coatings and materials that respond to changes in temperature or other environmental conditions. These developments are still evolving, but they point towards clothing that is an active, responsive system rather than a passive material.

## The sustainability question
Spacesuits are complex products containing many materials and components, so recycling them is far more complicated than recycling a simple cotton T-shirt. For future missions, however, sustainability could become essential, because transporting replacement clothing and equipment far from Earth is expensive and resource-intensive.

That makes three qualities especially valuable:
1. Longer-lasting garments
2. Repairable components
3. Recyclable materials

On future lunar or Martian settlements, researchers may even have to consider producing certain materials locally, which could lead to a completely new approach to textile manufacturing.

## From space to Earth
Research developed for extreme environments can eventually inspire technologies for use on Earth. High-performance fibres, thermal regulation, moisture management, protective coatings and smart textiles already have applications in sportswear, healthcare, defence and outdoor clothing. For example:
- athletes benefit from fabrics that help manage moisture and body temperature
- workers in hazardous environments need heat- and flame-resistant protective clothing
- medical textiles increasingly incorporate sensors and advanced materials

A technology first developed to solve an astronaut's problem may one day inspire a new category of performance clothing for consumers.

## Key takeaways
- Longer missions call for garments that are lighter, smarter, repairable and multifunctional.
- The AxEMU suit for Artemis III aims to fit at least 90 per cent of the US male and female population.
- Smart textiles could make clothing part of an astronaut's health-monitoring system.
- Self-healing, modular and recyclable designs could extend garment life and save resources.
- Research for space can inspire performance clothing on Earth.
