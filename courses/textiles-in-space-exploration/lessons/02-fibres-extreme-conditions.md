# Fibres built for extreme conditions
Ordinary cotton or polyester garments would not be suitable for a spacewalk. In this lesson you will meet the high-performance fibres used in spacesuits, see why each one is chosen for a particular job, and learn how the outer layers guard against wear and fast-moving particles.

## Why everyday fibres fall short
Cotton and polyester work well for daily clothing, but they are not designed for open flame, sharp metal edges, extreme heat or constant rubbing against hard equipment. Space textiles need specialised properties, so engineers turn to **high-performance fibres**, along with other engineered fibres and coatings.

## Aramids: strength and heat resistance
Among the most important high-performance fibres are the **aramids**. The name is short for *aromatic polyamides*, a family of synthetic fibres related to nylon but with a much stiffer molecular chain. Aramids are known for their strength and heat resistance, which makes them useful where conventional fabrics may fail.

Two well-known aramids are used in protective space applications:
- **Kevlar**, valued for its strength
- **Nomex**, valued for its flame resistance

## Ortho-Fabric: three materials in one
NASA has tested an outer spacesuit material called **Ortho-Fabric**. It is a good example of how engineers combine materials so that each one covers a different need.

| Component | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| Nomex | Flame resistance |
| Gore-Tex | Waterproofing and breathability |
| Kevlar | Strength |

None of these materials could do all three jobs on its own. Combined, they give an outer layer that is strong, resists flame and is still breathable, meaning it lets moisture vapour escape rather than trapping it inside.

## Vectran in the gloves
Gloves face a special hazard, because the hands touch sharp-edged surfaces and equipment more than any other part of the body. NASA has used **Vectran** in the palms of spacesuit gloves because of its **cut resistance**: the ability of a material to resist being sliced by a sharp edge.

## A system, not a perfect fabric
Different parts of a spacesuit need different properties, and these often pull in opposite directions. A material that gives excellent thermal protection may not offer the flexibility a glove needs. A highly flexible textile, on the other hand, may not provide enough protection against abrasion.

> **Key idea:** Spacesuit construction is less about finding one "perfect" fabric and more about engineering a textile system, with the right material in the right place.

This is the same thinking a garment technologist applies on Earth, only with far higher stakes. Each zone of the suit is matched to the hazard it faces and the movement it must allow.

## Tough outer layers
The outer layers of a suit take the most punishment. During a spacewalk, astronauts may come into contact with spacecraft surfaces, tools and other equipment, so the outer material must withstand repeated movement and friction. This property is called **abrasion resistance**: a fabric's ability to resist wearing away when it is rubbed.

### Micrometeoroids and orbital debris
Space may look empty, but it contains tiny particles travelling at enormous speeds. **Micrometeoroids** are very small natural particles of rock or metal, while **orbital debris** is human-made fragments left in orbit. Both can pose a serious risk to spacecraft and to astronauts.

A spacesuit therefore needs layers that resist penetration as well as abrasion, and this is another area where high-strength fibres are valuable. These protective layers are designed to reduce the risk from small particles while also coping with the everyday hazards of a spacewalk.

The difficulty is that designers must weigh this protection against weight, flexibility and durability, all at the same time. Adding more high-strength material improves protection but makes the suit heavier and stiffer, which brings us back to the central challenge from Lesson 1.

## Key takeaways
- Ordinary cotton or polyester garments are not suitable for a spacewalk.
- Aramids such as Kevlar and Nomex bring strength and heat resistance.
- Ortho-Fabric combines Nomex, Gore-Tex and Kevlar for flame resistance, breathability and strength.
- Vectran is used in glove palms because it resists cuts from sharp edges.
- Outer layers must resist abrasion and penetration by micrometeoroids and orbital debris.
