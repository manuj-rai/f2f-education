## Course page

**Learn how count, twist, strength, elongation, evenness and hairiness are measured, and what the numbers mean.**

Yarn quality shapes every later stage of textile production, from winding and weaving to how the finished fabric looks and performs. This free course, based on a Fibre2Fashion article by Dr Lipsa Mohapatra of NIFT Bhubaneswar, walks through the six properties that spinning mills and testing laboratories check as routine: count, twist, strength, elongation, uniformity and hairiness. For each one you will learn the definition, the units, how the test works and why the result matters in practice. Worked examples show how to find count from a lea, convert between count systems, and calculate twist multiplier, tenacity and elongation. A short quiz checks your understanding.

**Who it is for:** Spinning, weaving and knitting staff, quality-control trainees, textile and fashion students, and yarn buyers who need to read and question a yarn test report.

**You will learn to:**
- Explain how direct and indirect count systems express yarn fineness
- Calculate yarn count from a lea and convert between Ne, Tex, Denier and Nm
- Describe how twist is tested and use the twist multiplier to compare yarns
- Interpret strength and elongation results, including tenacity, RKM and CSP
- Explain how evenness and hairiness are tested and why they affect fabric quality

**Source:** this course is based on the Fibre2Fashion article [Yarn Testing: Count, Twist, Strength, Elongation, Uniformity and Hairiness](https://www.fibre2fashion.com/industry-article/11855/yarn-testing-count-twist-strength-elongation-uniformity-and-hairiness) by Dr Lipsa Mohapatra (NIFT Bhubaneswar) (Oct 2026).

## Lessons (one Video learning activity each)

### Lesson 1: Why Yarn Testing Matters (2 min 52 s)
_Six routine yarn tests, and the two systems used to express yarn count_

**Transcript**

Welcome. In this course we look at the yarn tests that spinning mills and laboratories run every day, starting with the one that underpins all the others: yarn count.
A fault that starts in the yarn rarely stays there. It travels downstream as machine stoppages and fabric defects, which is why yarn properties are measured as a routine part of quality control, not as an occasional check.
We will take each property in turn and ask the same four questions: what it is, how it is expressed, how it is tested and why it matters. The final lesson brings them together, because no single number tells you whether a yarn is good.
Why not simply measure the diameter? Because yarn is soft and compressible, so its diameter changes under the slightest pressure. Length and weight can be measured accurately, so every count system is built on those two quantities.
The quickest way to tell the two apart is to ask what happens to the number as the yarn gets thicker. In a direct system it goes up; in an indirect system it goes down. Mixing them up is an easy way to order the wrong yarn.
Because the length is fixed, a direct count is simply a weight. A 30 tex yarn weighs 30 grams per kilometre; double the weight for the same length and you double the tex. Denier works the same way over 9,000 metres.
Indirect systems count hanks instead. English count, used for cotton, asks how many 840-yard hanks make up a pound. Worsted uses a 560-yard hank, and metric count simply divides metres by grams, which is the same as kilometres per kilogram.
Every extra 840 yards packed into the same pound raises the count by one. A 40s yarn has twice the length per pound of a 20s yarn, so it is the finer of the two. The small s after the number is how English counts are usually written.
Keep that question in mind. In the next lesson we measure count in the laboratory and learn the conversion factors that let you move between systems with confidence.

### Lesson 2: Measuring Count and Converting Systems (3 min 50 s)
_Wrap reels, balances, and the factors that link Ne, Tex, Denier and Nm_

**Transcript**

Now we move into the laboratory. You will see how count is measured, why a lea weighed in grains gives such a neat shortcut, and how to convert one count system into another.
The method is simple on purpose. Fixing the length removes one variable, so the balance reading alone tells you the count. That is why some balances can be scaled to show the count straight away, with no calculation at all.
With a 120-yard lea weighed in grains, the numbers cancel neatly: 120 times 7,000 is 840,000, and dividing by the 840-yard hank leaves exactly 1,000. The whole calculation collapses to a single division.
Notice that 16,800 yards per pound is exactly the 20s line from the table in lesson one. A heavier lea would give a lower count, meaning a coarser yarn, which is just what you expect in an indirect system.
The factor 590.5 comes from turning 840 yards and one pound into metres and grams. Notice the pattern: between a direct and an indirect system you divide, because one rises as the other falls. Between two systems of the same kind you multiply.
Always finish with a check in the opposite direction. If you land back on the count you started with, the arithmetic is sound. Small rounding differences are normal, so quote results to a sensible number of figures.
The Beesley balance earns its place in fabric analysis. If a buyer hands you a swatch and asks what count was used, you can only unravel short pieces of yarn, and this balance is suited to exactly that situation.
Get the count wrong and everything built on it is wrong too: the twist multiplier, the tenacity, the CSP, and ultimately the weight and feel of the fabric the customer receives. That is why count is the starting point for most other yarn tests.

### Lesson 3: Twist: The Turns That Bind Fibres (3 min 25 s)
_Twist units, S and Z direction, twist multiplier and two ways to test_

**Transcript**

A loose bundle of staple fibres has very little strength. Twist is what turns that bundle into a yarn, and in this lesson you will learn how it is expressed, compared across counts and tested.
Twist works by pressing the fibres against one another, so friction stops them slipping apart when the yarn is pulled. The more firmly they are gripped, the more of their own strength the yarn can use, at least up to a point we will come back to.
Direction matters as much as level. When single yarns are plied together, the ply twist is usually inserted in the opposite direction, which helps the finished yarn stay balanced instead of snarling.
Yarn diameter shrinks as count rises, and yarns with the same twist multiplier end up with roughly the same fibre angle, so TM compares twist intensity across counts. A higher TM gives a harder, stronger yarn, but only up to a limit.
Turn it around and you can plan production. If a 36s yarn needs a TM of 4.0, the spinner sets about 24 turns per inch. For a 16s yarn at the same TM, the square root of 16 is 4, so only 16 turns per inch are needed.
Both methods use a twist tester fitted with a pre-tension weight and a revolution counter. Direct counting works when you can clearly see the components separate. In a single spun yarn you cannot, so the untwist–retwist method uses the change in length as its reference instead.
For example, if the counter reads 360 turns on a 10-inch specimen, the yarn had 180 turns in those 10 inches, or 18 turns per inch. Remember both divisions: first by two, then by the specimen length.
Past the optimum, each extra turn tilts the fibres further away from the direction of pull, so less of their strength resists the load. Extra twist also costs output, because every turn has to be inserted by the spinning machine.
Think about the optimum and the side effects on handle, bulk and hairiness. There is rarely one right twist, only the right twist for the end use.

### Lesson 4: Strength and Elongation (3 min 33 s)
_Breaking force, tenacity, RKM and CSP, and how far yarn stretches before breaking_

**Transcript**

Every yarn is pulled, bent and snatched on its way through winding, warping, sizing, weaving and knitting. This lesson covers how its resistance to those stresses is measured, and how to compare different yarns fairly.
Strength is the property that shows up most directly as lost time. Every break in winding or on the loom has to be found, mended and restarted, so weak yarn costs output long before anyone sees the fabric.
Breaking force on its own favours coarse yarns, which are stronger simply because they contain more fibre. Tenacity, RKM and CSP all bring count into the picture, so fine and coarse yarns can be judged on the same footing. RKM is numerically equal to tenacity in grams-force per tex.
The two tests answer slightly different questions. A single-end test shows the strength of individual lengths of yarn, while the lea test loads all 80 wraps together and gives one figure for the skein. That figure, in pounds, is what goes into the Count Strength Product.
Results from different principles are not always directly comparable, so a test report should state which type of tester was used. CRE is the principle behind modern single-end testers, so it is the one you are most likely to meet.
Elongation is defined against the original test length, so always note the length used. It is recorded together with strength, on the same tensile tester and in the same test, so one break gives you both numbers.
The RKM step converts centinewtons to grams-force, since one gram-force is about 0.981 centinewtons. Put another way, a 16.3-kilometre length of this yarn, hanging freely, would break under its own weight.
Two yarns can break at the same force and still behave very differently. The one that stretches further has a larger area under its curve, so it can absorb more of the sudden jerks a yarn meets on a loom or knitting machine.
Use the idea of work of rupture to answer. Strength tells you the peak load a yarn can take; elongation tells you how much shock it can absorb on the way there.

### Lesson 5: Uniformity and Hairiness (3 min 07 s)
_How consistent and how clean a yarn is, and how both are measured_

**Transcript**

Count and strength describe a yarn on average. Uniformity and hairiness look along its length and at its surface, where many of the faults that customers actually see in fabric begin.
No yarn is perfectly even, because the number of fibres in each cross-section varies. Evenness testing measures that variation, so that its causes can be found and kept within limits the end use can tolerate.
U% and CV% both describe overall variation, but CV% gives more weight to large deviations. For a normal pattern of variation, CV% is about 1.25 times U%. IPI is different: it counts individual faults you could point to on the yarn.
The percentages are sensitivity settings on the tester, measured against the average mass of the yarn. A thin place at minus 50 per cent has only half the normal mass at that point, which is why it is so likely to break.
A faulty rotating part leaves a fault that repeats at a regular length along the yarn, and the spectrogram shows it as a peak. The seriplane, by contrast, shows what the eye will see when yarn lies side by side, much as it does in fabric.
Faults like barré often become obvious only after the fabric has been woven or knitted and dyed, by which time the whole batch has been made. Catching unevenness at the yarn stage is far cheaper.
The H value has no unit because it divides centimetres by centimetres. It describes overall hairiness, while S3 counts only the longer hairs, which are the ones that tend to cause trouble in later processing.
Longer fibres leave fewer free ends along each length of yarn, and twist binds the ends that are there more tightly into the yarn body. Compact spinning condenses the fibre strand before twist is inserted, which traps more of those ends.
So the target is not zero hairiness but the right level for the end use. A crisp shirting needs a clean, clear surface, while a soft-handle fabric can benefit from some protruding fibre.

### Lesson 6: How the Six Properties Connect (3 min 13 s)
_Reading yarn quality as a whole, plus a quick reference for every test_

**Transcript**

You now know how each property is defined and tested. In this final lesson we put them side by side and see how they influence one another, because that is how a yarn behaves in a real mill.
Use this table as a checklist when you read a test report. If a property is missing, ask why; and when a figure is given, check that its unit matches the system you expect.
Notice how problems travel upwards. A count error distorts every figure calculated from it, and an uneven yarn shows up not only in its evenness report but also as lower strength and elongation, because a yarn tends to break at its weakest point.
This case is made up, but its logic comes straight from the way the properties connect. When one result looks fine and the yarn still misbehaves, look for the answer in a property that has not been checked.
That is the core message of yarn quality control. Count, twist, strength, elongation, uniformity and hairiness each give part of the picture; only together do they tell you how a yarn will run and how the fabric will look.
If the honest answer is only count and strength, you now know which questions to ask next, and which tests can answer them.
Those six points are the backbone of the course. The PDF guide repeats the formulas and the glossary, so keep it handy the next time you read a yarn test report.

## Glossary

- **Tex**: Direct count: weight in grams of 1,000 metres of yarn. A higher tex means a coarser yarn.
- **Denier**: Direct count: weight in grams of 9,000 metres of yarn. Denier = 9 × Tex; widely used for filament yarns.
- **English count (Ne)**: Indirect count: number of 840-yard hanks that weigh one pound. A higher Ne means a finer yarn.
- **Metric count (Nm)**: Indirect count: number of kilometre lengths that weigh one kilogram, i.e. metres per gram. Ne = 0.5905 × Nm.
- **TPI / TPM**: Turns per inch or turns per metre: the twist level in a yarn. TPM = TPI × 39.37.
- **S and Z twist**: Twist direction. With the yarn held vertically, the fibres slope like the middle stroke of the letter S or Z.
- **Twist multiplier (TM)**: TM = TPI ÷ √Ne. Expresses twist relative to count, so yarns of different counts can be compared.
- **Tenacity**: Breaking force divided by linear density, usually in cN/tex. Allows a fair strength comparison between yarns of different counts.
- **RKM**: Breaking length: the length of yarn, in kilometres, whose own weight equals its breaking force.
- **Count Strength Product (CSP)**: Lea strength in pounds multiplied by English count. Widely used in Indian spinning mills.
- **CRE, CRL, CRT**: Tensile tester principles: constant rate of extension, constant rate of loading and constant rate of traverse.
- **U% and CV%**: Evenness measures: U% is the mean deviation of yarn mass from the average; CV% is its coefficient of variation.
- **Imperfection index (IPI)**: Total of thin places (−50%), thick places (+50%) and neps (+200% for ring-spun yarn) per kilometre of yarn.
- **H value and S3**: Hairiness measures. Uster H: total cm of protruding fibre per cm of yarn. Zweigle S3: hairs 3 mm or longer per 100 m.
