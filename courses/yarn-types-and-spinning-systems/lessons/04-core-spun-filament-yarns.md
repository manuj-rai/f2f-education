# Core-Spun and Filament Yarns
So far, the yarns in this course have been made from staple fibres alone. This lesson brings continuous filaments into the picture: first as the hidden core of a core-spun yarn, then as the whole yarn. Both let manufacturers engineer properties into the yarn.

## What a core-spun yarn is
A **core-spun yarn** combines a **continuous filament core** with a **staple-fibre sheath**. In a typical modified ring-spinning process, the filament is introduced at the front roller, and the drafted staple fibres are twisted around it. Core-spun yarns can also be produced on rotor, air-jet and friction systems, as you saw with DREF-3.

The core can be:
- A **rigid filament**, such as polyester.
- An **elastic component**, such as **elastane**.
- A **bicomponent filament**, made of two polymers in one filament, such as PET/PTT (T400).

## How the core and sheath share the work
The core–sheath structure combines different functions in one yarn:

| Part | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| Core | Strength, dimensional stability or stretch |
| Sheath | Surface handle, appearance, comfort and dyeability |

A cotton sheath, for example, gives a cotton-like surface, while the filament core adds functional performance.

> **Key idea:** A core-spun yarn keeps the look and feel of a staple fibre while borrowing the strength, stability or stretch of a filament.

### Quality depends on process control
For **elastane-core yarns**, the draft applied to the elastane before it enters the yarn is critical. It affects count accuracy, twist, elongation and imperfections, and it can influence the tensile and tear performance of the fabric.

Two defects need particular control:
- **Core exposure**, or **grin-through**, where the core shows through the sheath.
- **Sheath slippage**, where the sheath fibres slide along the core.

### Dual-core and tri-core yarns
Recent developments use two or three core components, combining elastic with rigid or semi-elastic filaments to balance stretch, recovery and strength. Research on tri-core yarns with cotton sheaths and T400 and elastane cores has shown that the arrangement of the cores affects both yarn and fabric performance.

### Applications
- Polyester-core sewing threads
- Elastane-core stretch denim and woven fabrics
- Burn-out (devoré) fabrics, where a printed chemical removes the sheath in patterned areas and leaves the sheer core
- Protective and technical textiles

## Filament yarns
**Filament yarns** are made from one or more continuous filaments rather than staple fibres, so they need no conventional staple-fibre spinning.
- **Monofilament** yarn is a single filament.
- **Multifilament** yarn is many fine filaments grouped together, usually with little or no twist.

Most filament yarns are made from polymers such as **polyester, nylon, polypropylene and viscose**. **Silk** is the principal natural filament.

**Flat** filament yarns are smooth and compact. **Texturing** processes, such as **false-twist** and **air-jet texturing**, add crimp, loops or bulk to change stretch, cover and handle.

### How nylon filament yarn is made
Nylon filament yarn is produced by **melt spinning**:

1. **Polymer.** Nylon 6,6 is made by condensation polymerisation of hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid; nylon 6 by ring-opening polymerisation of caprolactam, with residual caprolactam removed by hot-water extraction.
2. **Drying and melting.** Chips are dried to a controlled moisture content, because moisture affects melt viscosity. A screw extruder melts them above the melting point: about 220 °C for nylon 6 and 260 °C for nylon 6,6. Continuous plants may feed melt straight to spinning instead.
3. **Extrusion.** A metering pump delivers the melt at a constant rate through the filters of the spin pack to the **spinneret**.
4. **Quenching.** Cross-flow air solidifies the filaments, and a spin finish is applied.
5. **Drawing.** For **fully drawn yarn (FDY)**, filaments pass from a feed godet (roller) to a faster, heated draw godet. The ratio of their speeds is the **draw ratio**. Drawing aligns the polymer chains and develops crystallinity, raising **tenacity** (strength relative to fineness) and reducing elongation. The heated godet also heat-sets the yarn.
6. **Interlacing.** An air jet entangles the filaments, giving cohesion in place of twist, before winding.

**Partially oriented yarn (POY)** is instead wound at high speed without full drawing, then drawn and false-twist textured later. **High-tenacity** nylon for tyre cord and industrial fabrics uses higher-molecular-weight polymer and higher draw ratios.

### Structure and properties
- **Uniform and strong:** with no staple-fibre ends and limited slippage between fibres, filament yarns are highly uniform and can be very strong.
- **Smooth surface:** flat filaments are lustrous with essentially no staple-fibre hairiness. They generally pill little but may **snag**, depending on fabric construction.
- **Feel:** synthetic flat filaments absorb little moisture and may feel smooth, cool or slippery; texturing adds bulk and improves handle.
- **Engineered:** fineness, cross-section, lustre and filament number can all be set during fibre production.

### Applications
| Filament form | Typical uses |
|---|---|
| Flat | Linings, lingerie, sarees, dress fabrics, curtains, umbrella fabrics |
| Textured | Hosiery, sportswear, activewear, knitted fabrics |
| High-tenacity | Tyre cord, seat belts, ropes, industrial fabrics |

## Key takeaways
- Core-spun yarn wraps a staple-fibre sheath around a filament core, usually on a modified ring frame.
- The core gives strength, stability or stretch; the sheath gives handle, appearance, comfort and dyeability.
- Elastane draft, grin-through and sheath slippage must be controlled for good core-spun quality.
- Filament yarns are continuous, so they are uniform, smooth, low in hairiness and potentially very strong; texturing adds bulk.
- Nylon filament is melt spun and drawn: FDY is drawn on heated godets as it is made, while POY is drawn and textured later.
