# Air-Jet and Friction Spinning
Rotor spinning is not the only way to make yarn quickly from sliver. Air-jet spinning uses swirling compressed air, and friction spinning uses the rolling action of two drums. In this lesson you will see how each one works and why each builds a very different yarn, with its own strengths and markets.

## Air-jet spinning: how it works
Air-jet spinning starts with drafting, as ring spinning does. The sliver passes through a roller drafting system, often with three or four rollers and **aprons**, short belts that guide the fibres through the drafting zone.

The drafted fibre strand then enters one or two **air nozzles**. Compressed air enters each nozzle tangentially, along its wall, and creates a swirling **vortex**.

### The twin-nozzle system
**Murata's MJS** (Murata Jet Spinner) was the first major commercial air-jet system. It uses two nozzles working together:

1. The **second nozzle** inserts **false twist** into the core of the strand.
2. The **first nozzle** swirls in the opposite direction and wraps edge fibres around that core.
3. After the nozzles, the false twist unwinds. As it does, the **wrapper fibres tighten** around the now parallel core.
4. Delivery rollers draw off the yarn, which is wound onto a package.

**False twist** is temporary twist. When a moving strand is twisted at one point, twist builds up before that point but cancels out after it, so none remains in the strand. In air-jet yarn, it is the tightened wrapper fibres, not twist in the core, that hold the yarn together.

### Vortex spinning
The later **vortex spinning system (MVS)** uses a **single nozzle with a hollow spindle**. It produces a higher proportion of wrapper fibres, which gives a more ring-like yarn.

## Air-jet yarn: structure and uses
Air-jet yarn is a **fasciated yarn**: a core of nearly parallel, largely untwisted fibres, held together by wrapper fibres at intervals. "Fasciated" means bound together in a bundle.

Compared with ring yarn, air-jet yarn has:
- **Low hairiness** and **good pilling resistance**.
- **Lower strength**, because the untwisted core relies on the grip of the wrapper fibres.
- A **stiffer handle**.

Air-jet spinning works well with **polyester**, **viscose** and **polyester-cotton blends**, and suits **medium to fine counts**. It provides the **highest delivery speeds** of the newer systems. Typical uses are shirting, sheeting, knitwear and home textiles.

## Friction spinning: how it works
Friction spinning is a true open-end system, best known through the **DREF** machines developed by Ernst Fehrer. It works like this:

1. Sliver is opened into fibres by a carding or opening roller.
2. An air stream carries the fibres into the **nip** between two **perforated drums**.
3. The drums rotate in the same direction. Suction through their perforations holds the fibres in the wedge-shaped nip between them.
4. Friction between the rotating drum surfaces **rolls the fibre assembly**, and this rolling inserts twist.
5. The yarn is withdrawn along the drum axis, at right angles to the direction of fibre feed.

Two machine types show the range of the system:
- **DREF-2** produces yarn from staple fibres alone.
- **DREF-3** adds a drafted **core strand**. Fibres from the friction zone wrap around it, producing **core-sheath yarns** with improved strength.

## Friction-spun yarn: structure and uses
Friction-spun yarn has a **core-sheath structure** with **uneven twist** from the core to the surface. Fibre orientation is poor, and fibres are often buckled or folded rather than lying straight. As a result, the yarn is **weak and bulky** and suits **coarse counts**.

Friction spinning has a narrow but important market:
- Coarse yarns, blankets, upholstery and filter fabrics.
- Technical core yarns, such as **heat-resistant yarns with aramid sheaths** and **conductive core yarns**.
- Yarns made from **waste and recycled fibres**.

## Three systems side by side
The three systems differ above all in how they insert twist, and that difference shows up in the yarn.

| System | How twist is inserted | Yarn structure | Typical counts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rotor | A rotating cup | Twisted core with irregular wrapper fibres | Medium to coarse |
| Air-jet | Swirling compressed air | Parallel core bound by wrapper fibres at intervals | Medium to fine |
| Friction | Rolling action of two drums | Central strand covered by wrapped fibres | Coarse |

These structural differences influence strength, handle and hairiness. All three produce yarns that differ from ring yarn, and none fully matches ring yarn in strength or softness. What they offer instead is higher productivity from sliver, plus yarn structures suited to particular end uses.

> **Key idea:** System selection depends on end use, fibre type and count range. No single system is best for every yarn.

## Key takeaways
- Air-jet spinning drafts the sliver, then uses swirling compressed air in one or two nozzles to wrap edge fibres around a parallel core.
- Air-jet yarn is fasciated: low in hairiness and resistant to pilling, but weaker and stiffer than ring yarn.
- Air-jet gives the highest delivery speeds and suits polyester, viscose and blends in medium to fine counts.
- Friction spinning twists fibres in the nip of two perforated drums; DREF-3 adds a core for stronger core-sheath yarns.
- Friction-spun yarn is weak and bulky, used for coarse yarns, blankets, filter fabrics and technical core yarns.
