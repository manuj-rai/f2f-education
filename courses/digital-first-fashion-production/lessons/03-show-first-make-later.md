# Show It First, Make It Later
If demand can be seen before an order exists, production could wait for it. This lesson looks at the first working versions of a “show first, make later” model, from virtual showrooms to 3D weaving, and at the limits set by shopper patience and distance.

## The first signs of the shift
Two practices already turn a forecast into something closer to a confirmed order:
- **Virtual showrooms (wholesale).** Store buyers view and order collections from 3D samples rather than physical ones, so the order book can take shape before sampling is complete.
- **Pre-orders (retail).** Some brands open pre-orders on new styles and commit to production only once enough customers have paid.

In both cases, the brand learns about real demand before it commits fabric and factory time.

## Made-to-order at the radical end
**Made-to-order** means a garment is produced only after a customer has bought it. The US company **Unspun** sits at the radical end of this idea. It began by selling custom-fit jeans made from body scans, and its founders found that measuring the customer was not the hardest part. The real problem was making a one-off garment affordably.

Their answer is a **3D weaving** machine that goes straight from yarn to garment. It skips three conventional stages:

1. Weaving flat fabric
2. Cutting it into pattern pieces
3. Sewing the pieces together

Walmart began piloting the technology in 2024, starting with men’s chinos, and Decathlon signed a partnership with the company later that year. Unspun’s aim is a network of small, local production sites that make clothing when it is ordered rather than months in advance.

There are limits so far. The garments produced have been simple woven styles such as trousers, so the technology is still some way from covering the full wardrobe.

## The patience problem
The main obstacle on the consumer side is patience. Shoppers have been trained to expect next-day delivery, and made-to-order clothing asks them to wait.

There is some evidence that they will wait if given a reason. A 2024 study of online fashion shoppers in German-speaking countries, published in the journal *Transportation Research Part D*, found that willingness to wait rose markedly when customers were offered savings on delivery or shown the emissions a slower option would avoid.

## Where made-to-order fits
Made-to-order is unlikely to suit every category.

| Better fit | Poorer fit |
|---|---|
| Fit matters a lot | Trend-driven pieces |
| Styles change slowly | Items bought on impulse |
| Denim, tailoring, workwear | The wait may lose the sale |

> **Key idea:** Show-first, make-later production suits garments where fit matters more than speed. For impulse buys, the wait can cost the sale.

## Distance adds to the wait
Much of the world’s clothing is made in countries such as Bangladesh and India, far from many of the shoppers who buy it. A factory there cannot make and post one jacket at a time at a sensible cost.

Instead, orders would need to be gathered into small batches before production starts, then shipped together once finished. Unless customers are buying on a clear pre-order basis and know they will wait, that delay is hard to justify.

## A wider risk for producing countries
There is a bigger concern too. Garments make up more than 80 per cent of Bangladesh’s export earnings, and the industry employs over four million people. Factories like these were built for volume.

If orders become smaller and less predictable, or move closer to Western markets, these factories could lose work, and the pressure on prices could grow. A shift to demand-led production would therefore have economic and social consequences far from the shopper’s screen.

## Key takeaways
- Virtual showrooms and pre-orders let brands see real demand before committing to production.
- Unspun’s 3D weaving goes from yarn to garment, skipping flat weaving, cutting and sewing.
- Shoppers are more willing to wait when offered delivery savings or shown the emissions avoided.
- Made-to-order suits fit-led, slow-changing categories such as denim, tailoring and workwear.
- Distant, volume-built factories face batching delays and the risk of losing work.
