# Digital Shoppers and Demand Signals
While production still runs on forecasts, shopping has moved on to screens. This lesson looks at the digital tools shoppers now use, why returns are such a costly problem, and how online behaviour could tell brands what to make before they make it.

## The shopper has already gone digital
A growing share of clothing is chosen on a screen, and the tools for choosing are becoming more sophisticated:
- **Virtual try-on.** In December 2025, Google brought its virtual try-on tool to shoppers in the UK and India, letting them see billions of apparel listings on themselves by uploading a single photo.
- **Smartphone body scanning.** The phone captures the shopper’s body shape and measurements.
- **Online size finders.** These recommend a size based on details the shopper provides.

Body scanning and size finders approach the same goal from a different angle. They turn a shopper’s body into data that can be matched against a garment.

## The returns problem
Retailers invest in these tools mainly because of returns. According to the EEA:
- Around **one in five** garments bought online is sent back.
- Around **70 per cent** of those returns come down to how the item looks or fits on the buyer.

Returns are not only a logistics cost. Before the EU ban on destroying unsold goods, the EEA estimated that between 22 and 43 per cent of returned clothing was destroyed rather than resold. Often, checking, cleaning and repackaging the item cost more than it was worth.

## Inconsistent sizing and bracketing
Part of the problem is that sizes are inconsistent. A “medium” from one brand can fit like a “small” from another, so many shoppers order more than one size and send the rest back.

This habit is called **bracketing**: ordering several sizes or colours of the same item to try on at home, then returning the ones that are not kept. A 2025 study of returns data from one of the UK’s largest online-only fashion retailers found that between 14.5 and 17 per cent of returns, depending on the department, came from bracketing.

> **Key idea:** Every returned garment was cut, sewn and shipped to meet a sale that never happened.

## Why returns matter to suppliers
Returns can look like a retail problem, but they start much earlier in the chain. The fabric, labour and transport behind each returned garment were spent on demand that turned out not to exist.

The information that would have prevented the return, such as whether the shopper was a size 10 or a 12, or whether she preferred navy or red, was captured only after the event. In a forecast-led system, that knowledge arrives too late to shape what the factory makes.

## Demand before the order
This is what is starting to change. Digital shopping can build a detailed picture of what people want, and what fits them, before they buy. Useful signals include:

| Signal | What it can reveal |
|---|---|
| Try-on sessions | Which styles and colours attract interest |
| Size selections | The real spread of sizes for a style |
| Abandoned baskets | Interest that has not yet become a sale |
| Wish lists | Demand that may turn into orders later |

All of these point to demand that has not yet turned into an order. For now, most of this information stays with retailers and their marketing teams. The question the article raises is what would happen if it travelled further up the supply chain, to the point where production decisions are made.

A brand that knew the likely size spread and colour preference for a style before cutting could order much closer to real demand. That is the idea the next lesson explores.

## Key takeaways
- Virtual try-ons, body scanning and size finders turn shoppers’ bodies and preferences into data.
- The EEA says about one in five garments bought online is returned, around 70 per cent of them for look or fit.
- Inconsistent sizing drives bracketing, which caused 14.5 to 17 per cent of returns in one UK retailer’s data.
- Try-ons, size selections, abandoned baskets and wish lists signal demand before an order exists.
- This data could guide production if it reached suppliers, not only marketing teams.
