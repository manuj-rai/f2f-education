# Small Batches and What Suppliers Need
Producing closer to demand sounds like producing less. This final lesson tests that assumption against the most successful demand-led model so far, then sets out five changes that demand-led production asks of mills and factories.

## Smaller batches, same volume?
Shein built its business on a **test-and-repeat** model. According to reporting by the Chinese business outlet 36Kr, first orders at its supplier factories typically run to just 100 to 200 pieces. The model works in three steps:

1. Release styles online in small quantities.
2. Watch sales data in real time.
3. Reorder at scale only the designs that perform.

In many ways this is the digital production model described in this course: design fast, show it to the market, and make more only once demand is proven.

The result, however, has not been a smaller fashion industry. The same model that keeps unsold stock low per style has let Shein release new designs at a pace traditional retailers cannot match, and grow into one of the largest fashion retailers in the world. A small first order does not mean a small total, because a style that sells can be reordered many times over.

For its suppliers, the model has meant a constant stream of short runs rather than fewer, larger orders. That pattern could spread as other brands adopt similar tools.

> **Key idea:** Digital tools can cut the waste that comes from guessing wrong, but they do not decide how much clothing the world buys. A system that tracks demand closely will track growing demand just as closely.

Whether digitalisation leads to more responsible production or simply faster consumption depends on how brands use it.

## What it asks of suppliers
For mills and factories, the direction of travel matters more than the pace. Even a partial move towards demand-led production changes what brands will ask for.

### 1. Smaller, faster, less predictable orders
Orders placed against confirmed demand tend to be smaller and more frequent, with less notice. A factory used to a handful of large seasonal orders may instead get a steady flow of short runs and repeat orders that depend on how a style sold that week. Planning, staffing and buying raw materials all become harder.

### 2. The economics of the minimum order
Short runs mean more **changeovers**, the work of switching a line from one style to the next, and every changeover costs time. Suppliers will need to decide how low their minimums can go, and how to price flexibility rather than absorb its cost. Factories that can switch lines quickly and still make a margin on a few hundred pieces will be better placed than those built only for volume. Accurate 3D patterns and automated cutting can help by reducing set-up time for each new style.

### 3. Holding fabric, dyeing later
If brands delay decisions on colour and quantity, the pressure moves upstream to the mill. One response is to hold fabric undyed and dye it once orders are confirmed, so stock can become whichever colour is selling. Mills that offer **stock-supported fabric programmes** can become valuable partners to brands trying to shorten their commitments. The trade-off is that the mill holds stock for longer, which ties up cash.

### 4. Data as part of the service
Demand-led production only works if information moves quickly in both directions. Shein, for example, gives its supplier factories digital systems that track order progress and sales data in real time. Suppliers that can work from 3D files, share live production updates and respond to sales signals will find this business easier to win. Over time, such capability may become a basic requirement rather than a way to stand out.

### 5. Distance and risk
Speed favours being close to the customer, which could strengthen the case for producing nearer to major markets. It also raises a harder question: when a brand stops guessing how much to order, who carries the risk? Somebody still has to hold fabric and keep capacity available. Suppliers will want that flexibility to be paid for, not simply passed down the chain. **Longer-term capacity agreements**, instead of one-off orders, may be one way to share the risk more fairly.

## From forecast to order
The jacket on the shopper’s phone is a small thing, but it signals a shift in where production decisions begin. For decades, the process started with a forecast and ended with a sale. Digital tools make it possible, at least for some products, to start with the sale instead.

Whether that becomes the norm depends on shoppers’ patience, the economics of short runs and how honestly brands use the data they gather. The suppliers best placed for this future will be those that can make small quantities well, quickly and profitably. Fashion has spent decades learning to make clothes at scale; the next test may be making them at the right scale.

## Key takeaways
- Shein’s test-and-repeat model starts with first orders of 100 to 200 pieces and reorders only proven styles.
- Demand-led production reduces waste from wrong guesses but does not by itself reduce total consumption.
- Suppliers face smaller, faster, less predictable orders and more changeovers.
- Mills may hold undyed fabric and dye it later, at the cost of tied-up cash.
- Flexibility should be paid for, for example through longer-term capacity agreements.
