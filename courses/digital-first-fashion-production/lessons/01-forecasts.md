# Why Fashion Produces on Forecasts
Most clothes are made long before anyone decides to buy them. This lesson explains why the fashion industry depends on forecasts, what happens when those forecasts are wrong, and how new EU rules are raising the cost of getting them wrong.

## Made first, sold second
Picture a shopper at her desk at lunchtime. She uploads a photo of herself to a brand’s product page and watches a knitted jacket appear on her 3D image. She checks the length against her hips, compares navy with red, settles on navy and orders.

The jacket does not exist yet: not in navy, not in her size and not in any warehouse. For now it is a 3D file made up of a pattern, a fabric simulation and a price.

For most of fashion’s history, the sequence has run the other way round. Clothes are designed, cut and sewn in their thousands, shipped across the world and put in front of shoppers in the hope that enough of them will sell. This course asks what changes if the sale comes first.

## Fashion’s educated guess
The mass-production model depends on **demand forecasting**: estimating, months before a collection reaches the shops, how many units of each style to make, in which colours and in which sizes.

These decisions are locked in early because of manufacturing economics:
- Fabric is bought by the roll.
- It is dyed by the batch.
- It is cut in stacked layers, many garments at a time.

Because the cost of setting up each step is shared across more pieces, the cost of each garment falls as the order grows. For the same reason, factories set a **minimum order quantity (MOQ)**, the smallest order they will accept. A production line that has to be reset every few hundred pieces loses time and money with each change.

The result is a system that makes clothes efficiently but still relies on guesswork: how many people will buy a style, like it and keep it rather than send it back.

> **Key idea:** Scale makes each garment cheaper, but it forces brands to commit to quantities long before they know what shoppers want.

## When the forecast is wrong
A forecast can miss in two directions:

| Forecast | What happens |
|---|---|
| Too cautious | The style sells out and the brand misses sales |
| Too optimistic | Surplus is marked down, moved through outlets and secondary markets, or never sold |

The cost of over-optimism is large. The **European Environment Agency (EEA)** estimates that between 4 and 9 per cent of all textile products placed on the European market are destroyed before they are ever used. That is between 264,000 and 594,000 tonnes a year.

## A ban on destroying unsold clothing
The rules are changing. Since 19 July 2026, large companies in the EU have been banned from destroying unsold clothing, clothing accessories and footwear. The ban comes under the **Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR)**, the EU law that sets sustainability requirements for products.

Three details matter:
- **Destruction is defined broadly.** It covers recycling and incineration as well as landfill.
- **Unsold items need another route.** They must be sold, donated or prepared for reuse.
- **Medium-sized companies get more time.** They have until 2030 to comply.

The ban does not stop a brand ordering too much. It does, however, give brands good reason to be more cautious, because surplus stock can no longer simply disappear.

## Why this opens the door to digital tools
If surplus is harder to get rid of, knowing demand earlier becomes more valuable. Online shopping is already a huge industry, 3D changing rooms exist, and brands can create digital garments realistic enough to sell before any fabric is cut.

That raises the two questions at the heart of this course. If a garment can be shown first and made later, could fashion move from producing on a forecast to producing on demand? And what would that mean for the mills and factories that make the clothes?

## Key takeaways
- Mass fashion production depends on forecasts made months ahead, by style, colour and size.
- Buying fabric by the roll, dyeing by the batch and cutting in layers make large orders cheaper per garment.
- Wrong forecasts mean missed sales or surplus; the EEA estimates 4 to 9 per cent of textiles placed on the European market are destroyed unused.
- Since 19 July 2026, large EU companies may not destroy unsold clothing, and destruction includes recycling and incineration.
- The ban makes knowing demand before production more valuable.
