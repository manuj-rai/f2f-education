# Unit 2: How spinning works
The spinning itself is quick. Most of the work in a spinning mill goes into preparing the fibre so that it can be spun evenly. In this unit you will follow fibre from the bale to the finished yarn, and compare the four spinning systems you will meet in mill specifications.

## From bale to yarn: the cotton route
1. **Opening and cleaning.** Compressed bales are broken open, and trash such as leaf and seed is removed.
2. **Carding.** Wire-covered rollers tease the fibres apart and form a loose rope called a **sliver**.
3. **Drawing.** Several slivers are combined and stretched together. This blends the fibres and straightens them.
4. **Combing** (for combed yarn only). The short fibres are removed and the rest are made parallel.
5. **Roving.** The sliver is drawn thinner and given a light twist so that it can be handled. This step is needed for ring spinning only.
6. **Spinning.** Final drafting and twist turn the roving or sliver into yarn.
7. **Winding.** The yarn is wound from small spinning bobbins onto large cones or packages, ready for weaving or knitting.

## The wool route
Wool starts with **scouring**, which washes the grease, dirt and sweat out of the fleece. Carding, combing (for worsted yarn), drawing and spinning follow. Wool fibres are covered in tiny overlapping **scales**, which help them grip one another when they are twisted.

> **Key idea:** Preparation differs by fibre, but the principle of spinning is the same for cotton, wool, flax and synthetic staple: draw the fibres out thin, then twist them so they hold together.

## The four main spinning systems

### Ring spinning
The oldest and most versatile modern system. Roving passes through pairs of rollers turning at different speeds, which draw it thinner. The yarn then runs through a guide and a small clip called the **traveller**, which runs round a ring as the bobbin turns. Each turn puts twist into the yarn. Ring spinning gives strong, fine, high-quality yarn across the widest range of counts, but it is relatively slow and needs more labour to keep running.

### Rotor (open-end) spinning
Sliver goes straight into a fast-spinning rotor, so no roving is needed. The fibres are thrown into a groove inside the rotor and twisted together as the yarn is pulled out through the centre. Rotor spinning is several times faster than ring spinning and highly automated. The yarn is bulkier, regular in appearance and cheaper, but weaker. It dominates coarser counts for denim, towelling and workwear.

### Compact spinning
A refinement of ring spinning. Just before the twist goes in, suction gathers the fibres into a tight bundle. This traps loose fibre ends, so the yarn is less hairy, stronger and more lustrous. Compact yarn is widely used for premium cotton shirting and knitwear, and many mills have converted ring frames to compact.

### Air-jet (vortex) spinning
Sliver is drafted and then twisted by a swirling jet of air instead of a rotating part. It is very fast and gives a smooth yarn with low hairiness that resists pilling. It is popular for viscose and polyester-cotton blends in knitwear. The trade-off is a slightly crisper, harsher handle and a narrower range of counts.

| System | Relative speed | Yarn character | Typical end uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ring | Slowest | Strong, fine, soft, some hairiness | Shirting, knitwear, sewing thread |
| Compact | Similar to ring | Stronger, smoother, less hairy | Premium shirting and knits |
| Rotor | Several times ring | Bulky, even, weaker | Denim, towels, workwear |
| Air-jet | Fastest | Smooth, low pilling, crisper | Viscose and blend knitwear |

Speeds are relative and vary with the machine and the count.

## S and Z twist
Twist can run in either direction. Hold the yarn upright: if the fibres slope like the middle stroke of a letter **Z**, it is Z twist; if they slope like an **S**, it is S twist. Most single yarns are Z twist. When singles are plied together, the ply twist usually runs the opposite way, which balances the yarn so that it does not snarl.

## Inside a worsted mill
Here is how a two-ply worsted yarn moves through a mill:

1. Blended, dyed wool goes through a multi-stage card. Each stage realigns and mixes the fibres so that the colour ends up even.
2. The card produces a very fine web, which leather tapes split into narrow strands.
3. The strands are rubbed into loosely twisted roving and wound onto spools.
4. Ring spinning draws the roving out and twists it into single yarn on ring bobbins.
5. Two singles are drawn through a guide and twisted together into a two-ply yarn.
6. The yarn is wound from the bobbins onto packages ready for weaving.

## Key takeaways
- Most of the work in a spinning mill is preparation: opening, carding, drawing, combing (for combed yarn) and roving.
- Ring spinning is versatile and gives strong, fine yarn, but it is slow; compact is a premium refinement of ring.
- Rotor spinning is fast and cheap and gives bulky, weaker yarn for coarse counts; air-jet gives smooth, low-pilling yarn at high speed.
- Most singles are Z twist, and plying usually uses the opposite (S) twist to balance the yarn.
