# Unit 3: Quality, specification and cost
Buyers rarely stand next to a spinning frame, but they read yarn specifications and test reports every week. In this unit you will learn to decode a yarn spec, predict how the fabric will behave, and explain where the cost of a yarn comes from.

## Five things decide yarn character
How a yarn looks, feels and performs comes down to five things: the **fibre**, the **count**, the **twist**, the **ply** and the **spinning route**. Change any one of them and the fabric changes.

### Fibre
Each fibre brings its own strengths. Wool gives warmth, nylon gives durability, cashmere gives softness, cotton gives comfort and breathability, and polyester and acrylic keep cost down. Within one fibre, longer and finer fibres spin into a better yarn.

### Count: how thick is the yarn?
Count describes the relationship between a yarn's length and its weight. Three systems are in common use, and they run in opposite directions.

| System | What it measures | Higher number means | Common in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ne (English cotton count) | Number of 840-yard hanks per pound | Finer yarn | Cotton trade, India, Bangladesh |
| Nm (metric count) | Metres per gram (km per kg) | Finer yarn | Wool, worsted, Europe |
| Tex | Grams per 1,000 metres | Coarser yarn | Technical textiles, man-made fibre (dtex for filament) |

Useful conversions: **tex = 590.5 ÷ Ne** and **Nm ≈ 1.69 × Ne**. So an Ne 30 cotton is about 19.7 tex, or Nm 51.

As a rough guide in cotton, Ne 6-16 is coarse (denim, towels), Ne 20-40 is medium (most T-shirts and casual shirts), and Ne 50 and finer is used for premium shirting.

### Twist: how tightly is it spun?
Twist is counted in turns per inch (**TPI**) or turns per metre (**TPM**). More twist holds the fibres more tightly, so the yarn becomes stronger and leaner, up to a point. Beyond that point the fibres start to shear against each other, the yarn weakens, and it feels harsh and lively.

Knitting yarns usually carry less twist than weaving yarns, for a softer fabric. Woollen yarns carry less twist than worsted because they hold more air.

To compare twist across counts, cotton spinners use a **twist multiplier (TM)**: **TPI = TM × √Ne**. A lower multiplier, often around 3 to 3.6, suits knitting; a higher one, often around 4 to 4.5, suits weaving warps. These ranges are approximate.

### Ply
A **single** yarn is one twisted strand. Twisting two or more singles together makes a **ply** yarn, which is stronger, more even and better balanced. The number of plies is written before the count: in cotton, "2/40s Ne" means two Ne 40 singles plied together, giving a yarn roughly as thick as an Ne 20. In worsted, "Nm 2/48" means two Nm 48 singles. **Cord** yarns twist plies together again, and **novelty** yarns use uneven feeding or twisting to create slubs, loops and other effects.

### Spinning route
As you saw in Unit 2, ring and compact give the strongest and finest yarns, rotor gives bulkier and cheaper yarns, and air-jet gives smooth, low-pilling yarns.

## How mills measure quality
The key measures on a yarn test report are:

- **Evenness (CV%):** how much the yarn's thickness varies along its length. Lower is better. Uneven yarn shows up as streaks in the fabric.
- **Imperfections:** counts of thin places, thick places and **neps** (small knots of fibre) per kilometre. They cause visible faults, especially after dyeing.
- **Hairiness:** how many fibre ends stick out. It affects pilling, lustre and sewing performance.
- **Strength and elongation:** how much force the yarn takes before it breaks, and how far it stretches. Weavers care most, because warp yarns are under constant tension.

Mills compare their results with published industry benchmarks, such as the **Uster Statistics** percentile tables. A useful buyer question is: "Where does this yarn sit against the benchmark?"

## Where yarn cost comes from
- **Fibre** is usually the biggest single cost. Expensive fibres are often blended with cheaper ones to reach a price.
- **Combing** adds a process and removes part of the fibre as waste, so combed yarn costs more than carded.
- **Spinning route:** ring is slower and needs more labour per kilo than rotor or air-jet, so it costs more to run.
- **Fineness:** finer counts take longer to spin per kilo and need better fibre, so cost rises steeply as counts get finer.
- **Ply and twist:** plying adds a process, and extra twist slows production.
- **Labour and energy** costs vary sharply by country, which shapes where each type of yarn is made.

> **Key idea:** Every cost saving at yarn level has a quality price somewhere: in handle, pilling, strength or appearance after washing.

## Cost scenario: the cheaper T-shirt
A retailer's best-selling basic T-shirt is made in Ne 30/1 combed compact cotton jersey. The buyer must cut the cost of the garment noticeably for next season, without customers noticing. Here are the options at yarn level:

| Option | Saves money? | What the customer may notice |
|---|---|---|
| Compact to conventional ring | A little | Slightly hairier surface, a little more pilling, less lustre |
| Combed to carded | Yes | Rougher, hairier, more pilling, less even appearance |
| Switch to rotor-spun yarn | Most | Harsher, bulkier handle and lower strength |
| Coarser count, lighter fabric | Yes | Thinner, less opaque fabric that may lose shape after washing |
| Blend in polyester | Yes | Less breathable, more synthetic feel, may pill; harder to recycle |

There is no single right answer. A defensible recommendation is to move from compact to conventional ring yarn and keep combed cotton, because it saves money with the smallest visible change, and then confirm the result with a wear and wash test before the order is placed.

## Key takeaways
- Fibre, count, twist, ply and spinning route together decide how a yarn and its fabric behave.
- Ne and Nm are indirect counts (higher is finer); tex is direct (higher is coarser).
- Test reports cover evenness (CV%), imperfections, hairiness, and strength and elongation.
- Fibre is usually the biggest cost; combing, ring spinning, finer counts and plying all add cost.
