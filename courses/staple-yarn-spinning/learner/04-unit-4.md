# Unit 4: Design, blending and sustainability
Yarn is where many design, cost and sustainability decisions are locked in. In this unit you will learn why fibres are blended, how colour effects are created at the yarn stage, and which questions to ask before you accept a sustainability claim.

## Why blend?
Blending puts two or more fibres into one yarn so that the product gets the best of each. Mills blend for four reasons:

- **Performance:** polyester adds strength and quick drying to cotton, nylon adds abrasion resistance to wool socks, and elastane adds stretch.
- **Production efficiency:** a stronger blend spins, weaves and knits with fewer breaks.
- **Handle and appearance:** viscose adds drape and lustre; wool adds warmth and bounce.
- **Cost:** a cheaper fibre brings down the price of an expensive one, as when cashmere is blended with wool.

## Intimate blends and mixtures
When fibres are mixed before spinning, so that each fibre's identity is lost in the yarn, it is called an **intimate blend**. A typical example is a 90/10 merino-nylon sock yarn. This differs from a **mixture** fabric, where separate yarns of different fibres are woven or knitted together, such as a cotton warp with a linen weft.

Blends have one important downside: they are much harder to recycle fibre-to-fibre, because the fibres must be separated again. This matters more as rules on circularity tighten.

## Colour at the yarn stage
Colour can go in at different points, and each gives a different look.

| Where colour goes in | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fibre dyed before spinning | Fibres of different shades blend into a speckled or heathered look | Mélange or grey marl sweatshirts, tweed flecks |
| Yarn dyed after spinning | Solid-coloured yarn for stripes, checks and jacquards | Shirting checks, Breton stripes |
| Two coloured singles plied | A twisted, two-tone marl | Marled knitwear |
| Yarn dyed in sections | The pattern appears when the yarn is woven or knitted | Ikat, space-dyed yarn |

> **Key idea:** Dyeing earlier gives richer effects, but it commits the colour sooner and adds lead time and minimum-order risk.

## Environmental impact
Spinning itself uses mainly electricity, for motors, air handling and humidity control. For most yarns, the larger environmental footprint lies upstream, in growing or making the fibre.

- **Cotton:** the impact depends heavily on how it was grown. Conventional cotton can involve high water use and heavy use of agrochemicals; organic and other certified programmes restrict these.
- **Wool:** the impacts sit in land use, animal welfare and the scouring stage.
- **Man-made fibres:** the impact depends on the feedstock (virgin or recycled) and the energy mix at the plant.

## Checking a claim: certification
Certification is how a buyer checks a claim. Each scheme certifies something different, so always check exactly what a certificate covers and which stages of the supply chain it includes.

| Scheme | What it covers |
|---|---|
| GOTS | Organic fibre content, plus environmental and social criteria through processing |
| OCS | Organic fibre content, tracked through the supply chain |
| GRS | Recycled content, plus social, environmental and chemical criteria |
| RCS | Recycled content, tracked through the supply chain |
| RWS | Wool from farms that meet animal-welfare and land-management standards |
| Better Cotton | Cotton grown to its standard; mostly sourced through mass balance, so it is not physically traced to your product |

Regulation is raising the bar. The EU's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation and its planned **digital product passport** will require better data on fibre content and origin. Forced-labour import rules in the US and the EU are driving demand for traceability back to the fibre.

## Social impact
Where yarn is spun shapes working conditions. Labour-intensive processes tend to sit in countries with lower labour costs, and spinning mills in South Asia have faced scrutiny over working hours, wages and the treatment of young women workers. Responsible buyers ask for audits and, increasingly, for visibility beyond their direct supplier, back to the spinner.

## Mini case: "Our new tee is sustainable"
A brand's marketing team wants to launch a T-shirt as "sustainable". The supplier says the yarn is "60/40 organic cotton and recycled polyester, ring-spun". Before you read on, list the questions you would ask before approving the claim.

### Questions a buyer should ask
- Which certificates cover the organic cotton and the recycled polyester, and do they cover the yarn and fabric stages too?
- Who is the spinner, and where? Can the fibre be traced back further?
- Can this blend be recycled at the end of its life? How does that affect the claim?
- What evidence supports the claim, and does it meet the green-claims rules in the market where it will be sold?

## Reflection

Identify one change at yarn level that would improve its quality, cost or sustainability, and explain the trade-off. Writing about 300 words is good practice for the applied assignment.

## Key takeaways
- Fibres are blended for performance, production efficiency, handle and appearance, and cost, but blends are harder to recycle.
- Colour added at fibre stage gives mélange and heather effects; yarn dyeing gives stripes and checks.
- For most yarns, the biggest environmental footprint is in the fibre, not the spinning.
- Certificates cover different things: check exactly what each one certifies before you accept a claim.
