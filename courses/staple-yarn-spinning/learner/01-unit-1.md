# Unit 1: What is a staple yarn?
Every fabric starts as yarn, and every yarn starts as fibre. In this unit you will learn the two basic ways to make a yarn, and the three grades of staple yarn that buyers meet every day. If you have not watched it yet, start with the one-minute video at the top of this unit.

## Two ways to make a yarn
Every yarn starts either as short fibres or as one continuous strand.

- **Staple fibres** are short. Cotton, wool, flax and most other natural fibres are staple, because they grow in short lengths: a few centimetres for cotton, longer for wool and flax.
- A **filament** is a continuous strand. Silk is the only natural filament. Man-made fibres such as polyester and nylon are extruded through a spinneret as filaments that can run for kilometres.

Short fibres only hold together as a yarn when they are twisted around each other. That twisting is called **spinning**, and the result is a **spun yarn** or **staple yarn**. Filaments can be used as they are, or chopped into short lengths and spun like cotton or wool.

## Why chop a filament into staple?
It sounds like extra work, so why do it? Because a spun yarn behaves differently from a smooth filament. The fibre ends that stick out of a spun yarn trap air and give a **softer, warmer, more natural handle** and better absorbency. That is why polyester appears as filament in a smooth lining but as staple fibre in a fleece or a polyester-cotton shirt.

## Pure yarns and blends
A **pure yarn** uses one fibre type, such as 100% cotton. A **blended yarn** mixes two or more fibres, such as polyester and cotton, to combine the strengths of each. You will look at blends in detail in Unit 4.

## Carded, semi-combed and combed
Before spinning, fibres are opened up and aligned by a machine called a **card**, which teases them into a loose rope called a **sliver**.

- **Carded yarn** is spun straight after carding. It still contains many short fibres pointing in different directions, so it is soft, bulky, slightly hairy and less hard-wearing.
- **Combed yarn** goes through an extra combing stage that removes the shortest fibres (called **noil**) and lines up the rest in parallel. It is smoother, stronger, more lustrous and more even.
- **Semi-combed yarn** sits between the two, with lighter combing and less fibre removed.

| Grade | How it feels | Typical uses | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carded | Soft, bulky, hairier | Denim, basic T-shirts, towels, muslin | Lowest |
| Semi-combed | Smoother than carded | Mid-range jersey and shirting | Middle |
| Combed | Smooth, strong, even, lustrous | Fine shirting, premium knitwear, lace, sewing thread | Highest |

> **Key idea:** Same fibre, different preparation, different product. Combing costs more because it adds a process and throws away part of the fibre.

## The wool and flax vocabulary
The wool trade uses its own words for the same idea.

| General term | In wool | Typical wool product |
|---|---|---|
| Carded | Woollen | Tweed jackets, chunky knits |
| Semi-combed | Semi-worsted | Mid-weight knits |
| Combed | Worsted | Suiting, fine knitwear |

**Woollen** yarn is lofty, warm and fuzzy. **Worsted** yarn is fine, smooth and crisp. Flax has its own terms too: **tow** is yarn spun from short flax fibres, and **line** is yarn spun from the long fibres.

## Why this matters commercially
The grade of yarn is one of the first things that separates a value product from a premium one. Two T-shirts in the same colour and weight can feel and wear very differently because one is carded and the other combed. A buyer who asks "carded or combed?" is already asking the right cost and quality question.

## Key terms
| Term | What it means | Typical product |
|---|---|---|
| Staple fibre | A short fibre that must be twisted to form a yarn | Cotton, wool, flax |
| Filament | A continuous strand of fibre | Silk, nylon, polyester |
| Carded yarn | Spun after carding only; soft and bulky | Denim, towels |
| Combed yarn | Short fibres removed and the rest aligned; smooth and strong | Fine shirting |
| Woollen yarn | The wool equivalent of carded | Tweed, chunky knits |
| Worsted yarn | The wool equivalent of combed | Suiting |
| Synthetic staple | Man-made filament cut short and spun | Socks, fleece |
| Mélange yarn | Fibres dyed different shades before spinning, for a speckled look | Grey marl sweatshirts |

## Key takeaways
- Staple fibres are short and must be spun (twisted) into yarn; filaments are continuous and can be used as they are or cut into staple.
- Carded yarn is soft and bulky; combed yarn is smoother, stronger and more expensive; semi-combed sits between.
- In wool, woollen means carded and worsted means combed; in flax, tow is short-fibre yarn and line is long-fibre yarn.
- "Carded or combed?" is a cost question and a quality question.
