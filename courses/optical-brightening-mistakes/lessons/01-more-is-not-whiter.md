# Why More Brightener Is Not Whiter
A white shirt gets about a second to pass a buyer's eye. If it shows even a faint cream cast, it looks old. That is why optical brightening agents appear in almost every recipe for white cotton, linen, polyester and blends. This lesson explains what these agents actually do, and why the familiar habit of "more is better" backfires with them.

## Bleach removes colour, brighteners add light
Even after bleaching, fabric usually keeps a little **residual yellowness**. Bleaching tackles colour chemically: it breaks down and removes coloured impurities. An **optical brightening agent (OBA)**, also called a fluorescent whitening agent, works in a completely different way. It adds light.

OBAs are **fluorescent**. They absorb invisible ultraviolet (UV) light at roughly 330–380 nanometres (nm) and give it back almost instantly as visible blue light at about 400–450 nm. The human eye sees light from about 400 to 700 nm, so the UV goes in unseen and blue comes out.

Blue is the opposite, or complementary, colour of yellow. The extra blue cancels the yellow cast, and because the fabric now sends back more visible light than it would without the agent, it also looks brighter.

Because the effect is optical rather than chemical, brighteners behave differently from bleach, and habits that work in a bleach bath often backfire in a brightening bath. Published research points to five common mistakes. This course works through all five, starting with the dose.

## Mistake 1: treating OBA like bleach
With hydrogen peroxide, a stronger bath usually gives a whiter fabric, at least until the fibre starts to be damaged. An OBA does not follow that rule. As the dose rises, whiteness climbs steeply, levels off and then falls.

A 2012 study by Hussain and co-workers measured this on cotton treated with a stilbene-type brightener. They reported the **CIE whiteness index**, a number calculated from instrument colour measurements in which higher means whiter:

| Brightener dose (% owf) | CIE whiteness index |
|---|---|
| None (untreated) | 80.6 |
| 0.15 | 145.5 |
| 0.25 | 144.1 |
| 0.50 | 140.0 |

**Owf** means "on the weight of fabric". A dose of 0.15 per cent owf is 150 g of brightener for every 100 kg of fabric.

The dose more than tripled between 0.15 and 0.50 per cent, and whiteness went down. Above the optimum, the extra brightener molecules cluster together on the fibre. Clustered molecules fluoresce less and start to absorb violet light themselves, so the fabric turns slightly green. The extra chemical made the fabric less white, and someone paid for it.

Other studies point the same way:
- Miljković et al. (2020) got the highest whiteness on cotton with a commercial brightener, Uvitex BHT, at just 0.1 per cent. That was almost twice the whiteness reached by hydrogen peroxide bleaching alone.
- Dekanić et al. (2020) found that the fluorescence of common brighteners in solution dropped sharply at concentrations of 2 per cent and above.

> **Key idea:** A brightener is not a bleach. Whiteness rises quickly at low doses, levels off and then falls, so the best dose is the lowest one that reaches your target, not the highest you can afford.

## Find your optimum with a dose ladder
The optimum depends on the brightener and on the fabric, so find it by trial rather than by habit. A **dose ladder** is a short series of trials at increasing doses:
1. Choose one OBA and one fabric quality.
2. Brighten samples at a few doses, for example 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.5 per cent owf.
3. Measure the whiteness of each sample. Lesson 3 explains how to do this reliably.
4. Lock in the lowest dose that reaches your whiteness target.

Run a ladder for each brightener and fabric quality you process. For most cotton brighteners, the optimum lies somewhere between 0.1 and 0.5 per cent owf.

## Key takeaways
- Bleaching removes colour; an OBA adds light by turning invisible UV into visible blue that cancels yellowness.
- With an OBA, whiteness climbs, levels off and then falls as the dose rises.
- Overdosing makes brightener molecules cluster, fluoresce less and tint the fabric slightly green.
- Run a dose ladder and use the lowest dose that meets the target, typically 0.1–0.5 per cent owf on cotton.
