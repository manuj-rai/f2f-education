# Controlling the Brightening Bath
Getting the dose right is only the start. The rest of the process decides how much of that brightener actually turns into whiteness, and how much steam and machine time it costs. This lesson covers three more mistakes: running the bath hotter and longer than needed, ignoring what else is in it, and trying to fix polyester whites with more brightener instead of more heat.

## Mistake 2: running hotter and longer than needed
Supplier data sheets have to work across many machines and water qualities, so their recommended conditions tend to be generous. One published comparison for a cotton brightener, Uvitex BHT, shows how much room there can be:

| Condition | Manufacturer's range | Shorter trial cycle |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 80–90 °C | 60 °C |
| Time | 60 minutes | 30 minutes |
| pH | 7–12 | Neutral |

The researchers, Dekanić et al. (2020), quoted the manufacturer's range and then deliberately ran the shorter, cooler cycle. They still obtained satisfactory whiteness on cotton, along with effective UV protection. Because OBAs absorb UV, brightened fabric also lets less of it through.

Every 20–30 °C and every half hour cut from a bath saves steam, water heating and machine time. Treat the data sheet as a safe starting point, not as proof that the full cycle is needed. Trial a cooler, shorter cycle against the data-sheet recipe on your own fabric and compare the results.

## Mistake 3: ignoring what else is in the bath
Most **anionic** (negatively charged) stilbene brighteners for cotton behave like direct dyes: the cotton takes them up directly from the bath. That makes them sensitive to whatever else the bath contains, and three things can quietly undo their effect.

### Cationic products
**Cationic** (positively charged) products work against these brighteners. Tutak (2011) found that cationised cotton, which has been chemically treated to carry positive charges, brightened to a lower whiteness index than untreated cotton. It also took on a blue-green rather than a reddish tint. Cationic softeners and fixing agents carried over into the OBA bath can cause the same kind of loss.

### Iron and other metal ions
Iron ions **quench** stilbene and biphenyl brighteners in solution, which means they reduce their fluorescence (Dekanić et al., 2021). Water quality therefore matters, and so does a suitable **sequestering agent**, a chemical that binds metal ions so they cannot interfere.

### Residual peroxide and alkali
An OBA can only add to the whiteness already achieved in pretreatment. Rinse and neutralise the fabric well after bleaching, before it goes into the brightening bath.

In practice, keep cationic finishes out of the brightening bath or apply them afterwards. And check the iron content of your process water before you blame the brightener.

## Mistake 4: dosing up instead of heating up on polyester
Polyester has no ionic sites, so it is whitened with a different kind of product. **Disperse-type brighteners** such as OB-1 behave much like disperse dyes. They only enter the fibre when it is heated well above its **glass transition temperature**, the point at which the polymer chains in the fibre loosen enough to let small molecules in.

Cai et al. (2023) optimised the whitening of polyester with a fine OB-1 dispersion and measured how much each factor mattered. Of the variation in whiteness:
- temperature accounted for 93.87 per cent
- time accounted for 2.07 per cent
- the amount of brightener accounted for just 1.65 per cent

Their best result came at 130 °C. The lesson for the shop floor is clear: more OBA cannot make up for a bath that does not reach temperature. If polyester whites come out dull, check the temperature profile before increasing the dose.

> **Key idea:** When whites disappoint, check the process before reaching for more brightener: carried-over cationics, iron in the water, poor rinsing after bleaching or, on polyester, a bath that never reaches temperature.

## Key takeaways
- Supplier data sheets are generous; trial a cooler, shorter cycle against them on your own fabric.
- Cationic softeners and fixing agents reduce OBA whiteness on cotton, so keep them out of the bath or apply them afterwards.
- Iron ions quench fluorescence, so check water quality and use a suitable sequestering agent.
- An OBA only adds to the whiteness pretreatment achieved, so rinse and neutralise well after bleaching.
- On polyester, temperature drives whiteness; fix the temperature profile before raising the dose.
