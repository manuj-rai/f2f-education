# Judging and Approving White Correctly
Even a well-made white can cause a dispute if people judge it under different light. This lesson covers the fifth mistake, sets out a dependable approval routine for process houses, quality teams and buyers, and shows why getting whites right also cuts cost and effluent. It ends with a checklist for the shop floor.

## Mistake 5: judging white under the wrong light
An OBA only works when ultraviolet light is present. Daylight and **D65**, the standard illuminant that represents average daylight, both contain **UV-A**. This is the part of the ultraviolet range closest to visible light, and it includes the band that brighteners absorb. Many warm LED and tungsten lamps contain very little UV.

That has real consequences:
- A shirt that looks brilliant at the window can look ordinary under shop lighting.
- Two lots can match in one light booth and differ in another.

The whiteness you see depends directly on the UV content of the light falling on the fabric. For the same reason, measuring whiteness with an instrument relies on careful control of the UV content of the instrument's own light source, a point stressed by the colour measurement company Datacolor.

## A dependable approval routine
1. **Look under D65.** Compare samples side by side in a light cabinet under D65.
2. **Check under a UV lamp.** Uneven brightener shows up as streaks or patches.
3. **Measure with UV included.** Use a spectrophotometer, an instrument that measures colour wavelength by wavelength, that includes the UV component and is calibrated with a fluorescent standard. Follow ISO 105-J02 or AATCC TM110.
4. **Keep the UV setting calibrated.** Recalibrate it regularly; Datacolor recommends weekly.
5. **Report two numbers.** Record both the CIE whiteness index and the whiteness tint.

For buyers and suppliers, agreeing this routine in advance means both sides are judging the same thing, under the same light.

## Reading the whiteness tint
The **whiteness tint (Tw)** shows which way a white leans (Tutak, 2011):
- Between −3 and +3, the white is considered neutral.
- Positive values indicate a greenish tint.
- Negative values indicate a reddish tint.

Remember from Lesson 1 that overdosed fabric turns slightly green. A rising Tw is therefore an early warning of overdosing, which is why it belongs in every report alongside the whiteness index.

> **Key idea:** Approve whites under D65 with a UV-calibrated spectrophotometer, and track tint as well as whiteness. A rising tint warns that the dose has gone past its optimum.

## Why it matters beyond the fabric
OBAs are designed to be stable, which makes them hard to remove from wastewater. Poiger et al. (1996) measured the two most common detergent brighteners at:
- 7–21 µg/L (micrograms per litre) in primary sewage effluent
- 2.6–8.9 µg/L after secondary treatment
- 0.04–0.57 µg/L in river water

Brighteners are highly water-soluble and poorly biodegradable, and much of them passes through biological treatment (Assaad et al., 2014).

Any brightener that stays in the bath instead of on the fabric is chemical you paid for and then sent down the drain, where it adds to the effluent load. The good news is that the steps that give the best whiteness also give the lowest cost and the smallest environmental footprint: the right dose, high **exhaustion** (the share of brightener that moves from the bath onto the fabric), a clean bath and the correct temperature.

## The five mistakes at a glance

| Mistake | What happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Dosing OBA like bleach | Molecules cluster; whiteness falls and the fabric turns greenish | Run a dose ladder; use the lowest dose that meets the target |
| Running hotter and longer than needed | Steam, water heating and machine time are wasted | Trial a cooler, shorter cycle against the data sheet |
| Ignoring the rest of the bath | Cationic carry-over and iron cut whiteness; poor pretreatment limits it | Keep cationics out, control iron, rinse and neutralise |
| Adding OBA to under-heated polyester | Dull whites that more brightener cannot fix | Fix the temperature profile first |
| Judging under the wrong light | Whites look different at the window, in shops and between booths | Approve under D65 with a UV-calibrated instrument |

## Quick checklist
1. Run a dose ladder and adopt the lowest dose that meets the target, typically 0.1–0.5 per cent owf on cotton.
2. Trial a shorter, cooler cycle against the data-sheet recipe.
3. Keep cationic chemicals out of the OBA bath and control iron in the process water.
4. On polyester, sort out the temperature profile before raising the dose.
5. Approve whites under D65 with a UV-calibrated spectrophotometer, tracking both whiteness index and tint.

## Key takeaways
- OBAs need UV to work, so whites look different under lamps with different UV content.
- Judge under D65, check under a UV lamp for streaks, and measure with a UV-calibrated spectrophotometer.
- Report both the CIE whiteness index and the whiteness tint; a rising tint warns of overdosing.
- Brighteners are stable and pass through treatment, so unused brightener means wasted money and extra effluent load.
- The right dose, high exhaustion, a clean bath and the correct temperature give the best white at the lowest cost.
