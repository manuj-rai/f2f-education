# Producer Countries and Getting Ready
Most of the world’s clothing is made far from the markets that write the strictest rules. This final lesson looks at the policy gap between consumer markets like the EU and producer countries in Asia, and then sets out practical steps brands and suppliers can take to prepare.

## The energy behind a garment
Textile manufacturing is highly energy-intensive, and the energy mix matters as much as the amount. Globally, most textile production still relies on natural gas and thermal energy, coal and oil remain dominant, and renewable sources account for **less than 10 per cent** of energy generation.

Approximate energy use at each stage:

| Process | Energy use |
|---|---|
| Spinning | About 25-30 kWh per 100 kg of yarn |
| Weaving | About 25-30 kWh per 100 kg of fabric |
| Dyeing and finishing | About 200-300 kWh per 100 kg of fabric |

These figures vary by country, machinery and process, but the pattern is clear: wet processing uses far more energy than spinning or weaving. Energy is also a cost issue. In Bangladesh, for example, the ongoing energy crisis contributes significantly to high production costs, which is why the article argues for policies that support renewable energy.

## Asia’s energy picture
The article’s comparison of three major producers shows both the challenge and the progress.

| Country | Current reliance | Renewable target |
|---|---|---|
| India | About 48 per cent of energy from coal | Around 500 GW of renewable energy by 2030 |
| Vietnam | About 55 per cent from fossil fuels | 4,000 MW of solar energy by 2025 |
| Bangladesh | About 98 per cent from fossil fuels | 2.3 GW of solar energy by 2030 |

India, Bangladesh and Vietnam have all adopted solar energy significantly. In Vietnam, garment factories have increasingly turned to solar power to meet the demands of markets such as the European Union. This is a clear example of a consumer market’s expectations shaping how goods are made thousands of kilometres away.

> **Key idea:** EU policy reaches far beyond Europe. Suppliers that sell to European brands feel its effects through buyer requirements long before any law applies to them directly.

## The policy gap
The article describes a **significant policy gap**. The EU is implementing stringent regulations, while many Asian apparel producers face structural challenges that make similar progress difficult. The main obstacles it identifies are:
- **informal waste collection**, which keeps used textiles outside formal recycling systems;
- **poorly integrated recycling systems** that need a complete overhaul;
- limited visibility of existing value chains to institutions, investors and banks, which restricts access to finance.

The article argues that many Asian countries already have the foundations of their textile systems in place. Formally recognising these value chains would make them more attractive for investment. It calls for closer coordination between producer and consumer countries to develop more responsible and effective policies, and sees the push for sustainability as an opportunity for Asian producers to take a leading role.

## Getting ready: five steps for brands
The EPR article sets out five ways brands can prepare for compliance.

1. **Adopt sustainable materials and circular design.** Favour recyclable, mono-material fabrics and work with researchers on next-generation textiles.
2. **Develop take-back and recycling programmes.** Most brands lack the infrastructure to process returned clothing, so partnerships with recyclers and upcyclers are essential.
3. **Educate consumers and encourage participation.** Offer incentives such as store credit or loyalty points for returned garments, and explain responsible disposal through campaigns and in-store information.
4. **Collaborate with policymakers and peers.** Support harmonised EPR rules and join industry initiatives such as the Ellen MacArthur Foundation’s Make Fashion Circular programme or Cascale, formerly the Sustainable Apparel Coalition.
5. **Invest in digital solutions.** Use traceability systems to follow products from production to disposal, and data analytics to understand waste, returns and inventory.

## What this means for suppliers
Although EPR obligations fall on the brands that sell products, suppliers are part of every step above. Buyers will look for mono-material and recyclable fabric options, reliable information on fibre content, and cleaner energy in production. Suppliers who can provide these will be better placed as policy tightens.

## Key takeaways
- Textile production is energy-intensive, and dyeing and finishing use the most energy.
- India, Vietnam and Bangladesh still rely heavily on fossil fuels but have set renewable energy targets.
- Buyer expectations from markets like the EU are already changing practices in producer countries.
- A policy gap exists between strict EU rules and the structural challenges facing Asian producers.
- Brands can prepare through circular design, take-back, consumer education, collaboration and digital tracking.
