# EPR Laws Around the World
Textile EPR is not one law but a patchwork of national and regional schemes. Only a few countries had fully working systems when the source article was written, while many more were designing them. This lesson compares the main schemes and explains why the differences matter for anyone who sells or sources across borders.

## France: the pioneer
France was the first country to introduce textile EPR, with legislation as early as 2007. Its scheme was broadened significantly in 2020 by the **AGEC law** (France’s anti-waste law for a circular economy), and now covers a broad range of textile goods, including garments, shoes and household linens.

How the French system works:
- Businesses pay fees to **Refashion**, the national EPR body.
- Fees are calculated on the **volume and nature** of the products sold, and adjusted to encourage sustainable and recyclable materials.
- Brands must report on sales volumes, recycled content and end-of-life management.
- Products must carry the **Triman** label, which tells consumers how to dispose of them properly.
- The system is supported by a large national network of textile collection points.

France shows all four building blocks of EPR working together: collection, eco-modulated fees, targets and reporting.

## The Netherlands, Latvia and Hungary
Several other European countries followed with schemes of their own, each with a different design.

| Country | Started | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Netherlands | 1 July 2023 | Producers register and report the weight of textiles they place on the market each year |
| Latvia | 1 July 2024 | A Natural Resource Tax on textile imports, unless a company joins an approved collective EPR organisation |
| Hungary | 2023 legislation | Producers comply on their own or join a certified producer responsibility organisation |

The Dutch scheme also sets clear targets: at least **50 per cent** of textiles placed on the market should be prepared for reuse or recycling by 2025, rising to **75 per cent by 2030**.

A **producer responsibility organisation (PRO)** is a collective body that takes on EPR obligations, such as collection and reporting, on behalf of its member companies. For many brands, joining a PRO is simpler than running a scheme alone.

## The European Union
In July 2023 the European Commission proposed amendments to the **Waste Framework Directive** that would require every member state to adopt textile EPR systems within 18 to 30 months of the directive coming into force. The proposal includes **tiered fee structures** designed to discourage environmentally harmful practices such as fast fashion.

Separately, EU member states were required to roll out dedicated **separate collection of textile waste** by January 2025, although progress has varied from country to country.

## California
In the United States, **California** became the first state to adopt mandatory textile EPR. Its **Responsible Textile Recovery Act**, enacted in September 2024, requires producers to enrol in a stewardship organisation approved by CalRecycle, the state’s recycling agency, by January 2026.

The programme covers apparel, home textiles and accessories, while second-hand retailers and smaller businesses are exempt. Producers must meet recycling targets and pay into fee structures designed to promote sustainability.

## Countries still exploring
Other countries were exploring or piloting textile EPR when the article was written, including **Australia, Colombia, South Korea and the United Kingdom**. In the United States, proposed laws in **Washington** and **New York** aim to create similar mandates.

> **Key idea:** Policy moves quickly. The dates and stages in this lesson are as reported in May 2025. Before making compliance decisions, check the current rules in each market you sell to.

## Why the differences matter
For a brand or supplier working in one country, one set of rules is manageable. For a global business, the differences add up. A company selling in France, the Netherlands and California may face three different fee systems, registration processes and reporting formats.

The article highlights two consequences:
- **Operations must adapt market by market.** Brands need to adjust their compliance, logistics and reporting to each country’s requirements.
- **Harmonisation becomes a business interest.** Because the lack of uniform rules creates compliance challenges, brands have good reason to take part in regulatory discussions and support more consistent EPR standards across regions.

## Key takeaways
- France pioneered textile EPR in 2007 and expanded it through the 2020 AGEC law, with fees paid to Refashion.
- The Netherlands, Latvia and Hungary run schemes with different designs, from annual reporting to import taxes.
- The Dutch scheme targets 50 per cent reuse or recycling by 2025 and 75 per cent by 2030.
- The EU proposed EPR for all member states, with tiered fees to discourage fast fashion.
- California was the first US state with textile EPR; differing rules make harmonisation valuable for global brands.
