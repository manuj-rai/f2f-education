# Designing and Recycling for Circularity
Policy sets the rules, but products and recycling systems have to change to meet them. This lesson looks at how EPR pushes design towards recyclability, what closed-loop recycling can and cannot do today, and how take-back programmes and digital tracking fit into the picture.

## Design is where the cost is decided
Eco-modulation fees reward products that are easier to recycle and penalise those that are not. That links a garment’s end-of-life cost directly to choices made at the design stage. The article highlights three shifts.

### Mono-materials over blends
Blended fabrics are hard to recycle, because separating synthetic and natural fibres requires energy-intensive processes. EPR is expected to increase demand for **mono-material** fabrics, such as 100 per cent organic cotton or pure polyester, which recycling systems can handle more easily.

### Repairable and modular design
Garments that are easy to repair, alter or take apart last longer and are simpler to recycle at the end of their life. EPR encourages modular and repairable designs because they extend product lifespans and reduce overall waste.

### Lower-impact materials
The article also points to biodegradable and next-generation alternatives, such as bio-based polyester and innovative fibres like Tencel and Piñatex, and suggests brands work with material scientists and research institutions on new sustainable textiles. Each new material should still be judged on how it performs in real collection and recycling systems.

## What happens to discarded clothing today
At present, discarded clothing is often **exported as second-hand garments** or **downcycled**: recycled into products of lower value, such as rags or insulation. Technologies for turning old clothing back into new apparel are still developing, largely because garments are made from such a wide variety of fibre types.

The article also notes that the industry’s capacity for circular practices remains underused, mainly because of insufficient investment and a lack of strong government support.

## Closed-loop recycling
**Closed-loop recycling** is the approach many countries are now considering. In a closed-loop system, the materials used to make garments come from recycling collections or take-back schemes. A crucial requirement is **quality**: a recycled garment must produce a new garment of comparable quality to the original.

How far has it come?
- The only successful pilot so far has been for **polyester** garments, the most widely used fibre type globally.
- Some brands recycle their post-consumer waste into new garments.
- No company or country has yet developed a foolproof way to make completely recycled garments.

### A case study: Wear2Wear
In the **Wear2Wear** initiative, leading European brands collaborate across the textile value chain to make apparel from recycled materials, with transparency and traceability built in so that clothing can be fully recycled at the end of its life. Instead of making polyester from petroleum, the process starts with recycled PET bottles.

A life cycle analysis compared Wear2Wear with traditional linear production.

| Impact | Change with Wear2Wear |
|---|---|
| Environmental pollution | About 25 per cent lower |
| Greenhouse gas emissions | About 33 per cent lower |
| Water use | About 45 per cent lower |
| Human health risks | About half |

The results vary with the fibre type recycled and the garment produced, so each recycling method needs to be assessed on its own rather than assumed to perform the same.

> **Key idea:** Recycling only closes the loop if the output is good enough to become a new garment. Designing for recyclability makes that much more likely.

## Take-back and recycling programmes
Under EPR, many brands are expected to build in-house recycling and repair programmes and to partner with **textile-to-textile recyclers**, the companies developing ways to regenerate fibres from old garments. H&M and Zara already run garment collection initiatives, although the article notes that EPR will require more substantial action. Adidas has developed fully recyclable shoes, and Patagonia’s Worn Wear programme shows how a brand can involve customers in repair and reuse.

Collection also creates logistics challenges: brands need systems to collect, sort and redistribute used clothing, and investment in sorting technology to reduce contamination.

## Digital tracking and transparency
EPR depends on information. Brands must disclose the material content and recyclability of products and tell customers how to dispose of them responsibly. To support this, the EU is developing a **Digital Product Passport** system to track textile recycling and make a product’s life cycle more transparent.

## Key takeaways
- Eco-modulation links end-of-life costs to design, favouring mono-materials and repairable garments.
- Much discarded clothing is still exported second-hand or downcycled.
- Closed-loop recycling requires new garments of comparable quality; only polyester has a successful pilot.
- Wear2Wear’s life cycle analysis showed lower pollution, emissions and water use than linear production.
- Take-back programmes, recycling partners and the EU Digital Product Passport support EPR compliance.
