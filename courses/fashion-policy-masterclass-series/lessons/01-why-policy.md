# Why Fashion Needs Policy
For most of its history, the fashion industry has had little responsibility for what happens to a garment once it is sold. That is changing fast. This lesson explains why governments, led by the European Union, are moving fashion from voluntary pledges to binding rules, and what those rules are trying to fix.

## From voluntary pledges to binding rules
Traditionally, a brand’s responsibility ended at the till. What happened next, whether the garment was worn, resold, recycled or thrown away, was a matter for the consumer and for public waste services. The costs of dealing with discarded clothing sat outside the brand’s accounts.

Policy is now changing that. Instead of relying on brands to make sustainability promises, governments are writing rules that apply to everyone who sells into their markets. The European Union is the clearest example, and its rules affect suppliers far beyond Europe.

## The European Green Deal
The **European Green Deal** is one of the EU’s most ambitious policies. Its aim is to make the bloc green by 2050. Along the way, it sets a target to **cut emissions by 55 per cent by 2030**.

The Green Deal is not a single law. It is a cross-sector framework designed to align every part of the economy, from manufacturing to finance, behind the same goal. It also aims for a transition that is cost-effective and socially fair, so that no industry or group of workers is simply left behind.

For textiles, this matters because the industry touches almost every part of that framework: energy use in factories, chemicals in processing, transport, retail and waste.

## The Circular Economy Action Plan and the Textile Strategy
Under the Green Deal sits the **Circular Economy Action Plan (CEAP)**. A **circular economy** keeps materials in use for as long as possible through reuse, repair and recycling, instead of the linear model of make, use and throw away.

For textiles, the CEAP promotes sustainable use of resources. Its key measures include:
- improving **product design** so garments last longer and can be recycled;
- supporting the use of **recycled materials**;
- encouraging reuse and recycling, and minimising waste;
- reducing the overall environmental impact of textiles.

The CEAP is closely aligned with the **EU Textile Strategy**, which addresses the sustainability challenges specific to the sector.

Why focus on textiles at all? The figures cited in the source article make the case.

When almost all discarded clothing is not recycled, the materials, water, energy and labour that went into it are largely lost. EU policy openly aims to push **fast fashion**, the model of cheap, quickly changing and short-lived garments, out of the economy.

> **Key idea:** EU textile policy is aimed at the whole life of a garment, not just how it is made. Design, use, collection and recycling all fall within its scope.

## Making producers responsible
One of the most important tools in this shift is **Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)**. Under EPR, producers become responsible for what happens to their products after use, including buyback, reuse and recycling of the clothing that is collected.

Some brands have already started to prepare. The article notes that H&M, for example, has been taking steps with in-store recycling technology such as its Looop system. The next lesson looks at how EPR works in detail.

## Policy and reality
Good policy on paper does not guarantee change on the ground. The article points out that the EU, despite its ambitious sustainability policies, still depends heavily on natural gas for energy. A balance is needed between what is announced as policy and what is actually happening. We return to this gap in the final lesson, when we look at producer countries in Asia.

## Key takeaways
- Fashion brands have traditionally carried little responsibility for garments after sale; policy is now changing that.
- The European Green Deal aims for a green EU by 2050 and a 55 per cent emissions cut by 2030.
- The Circular Economy Action Plan and EU Textile Strategy push better design, recycled materials, reuse and less waste.
- Around 5 million tonnes of clothing are discarded in the EU, and only about 1 per cent is recycled.
- Extended Producer Responsibility is a central tool for making producers responsible for their products’ end of life.
