# Solution Spinning: Dry and Wet Routes
Some polymers break down before they melt, so they cannot be melt spun. This lesson shows how dissolving the polymer gets round that problem, how dry and wet spinning remove the solvent in different ways, and how two further variants are used to make high-performance fibres.

## When the polymer cannot be melted
**Solution spinning** is used when a polymer cannot be melted without decomposition. Instead, the polymer is dissolved in a suitable solvent to form a thick, viscous spinning solution called the **dope**.

The dope is extruded through a spinneret just as a melt would be. The difference lies in how the filament becomes solid: it solidifies as the solvent is removed. The way the solvent is removed is what separates **dry spinning** from **wet spinning**.

## Dry spinning: evaporate the solvent
In dry spinning, the dope contains a **volatile solvent**, one that evaporates easily. The process runs like this:

1. The dope is extruded into a heated chamber through which hot air or an inert gas flows.
2. The solvent evaporates from the filaments, and they solidify.
3. The solvent vapour is recovered for reuse.

Typical dry-spun fibres include **cellulose acetate**, **triacetate**, some **acrylic** fibres and **elastane** (spandex).

### Effect on cross-section
Because solidification begins at the filament surface, the cross-section may become non-circular. A solid skin forms first, and as solvent then escapes from the inside, the filament shrinks and its outline changes. Acetate fibres can show a **lobed** outline, while dry-spun acrylic may develop a **dog-bone** shape.

### Speed and cost
Dry spinning is slower than melt spinning, because the rate of solvent evaporation controls how quickly the filament solidifies. Solvent recovery also adds to process cost and calls for appropriate safety and environmental controls.

## Wet spinning: coagulate in a bath
In wet spinning, the spinneret sits directly in a **coagulation bath**. As the filaments emerge, they solidify through **solvent exchange** (solvent leaves the filament while the bath liquid moves in) or through a **chemical reaction**. They are then washed, drawn, finished and dried. The product may be wound as continuous filament or cut into staple fibre.

### Example: viscose rayon
Viscose rayon is a major wet-spun fibre, and its production shows the chemical-reaction route clearly:

1. Cellulose is converted to **sodium cellulose xanthate**.
2. The xanthate is dissolved in dilute sodium hydroxide to make the dope.
3. The dope is extruded into an **acid bath**, usually containing sulphuric acid, sodium sulphate and zinc sulphate.
4. In the bath, cellulose is **regenerated**: it is turned back into cellulose, now in filament form.

The **serrated cross-section** of viscose is associated with skin formation and shrinkage during regeneration.

### Other wet-spun fibres
Acrylic fibres may also be wet spun, using solvents such as **dimethylformamide (DMF)** or **dimethylacetamide (DMAc)**. Modacrylic fibres are produced by this route too.

### Speed and cost
Wet spinning is the **slowest** of the three principal methods, because coagulation depends on chemical and diffusion processes. It also involves handling large volumes of liquid for coagulation, washing and chemical recovery.

> **Key idea:** Dry spinning removes solvent by evaporation in hot gas; wet spinning solidifies the filament by coagulation in a bath. Both need solvent or chemical recovery.

## Two routes to high-performance fibres

### Dry-jet wet spinning
Here the spinneret is placed a short distance **above** the coagulation bath, so the extruded filament passes through a small **air gap** before it enters the bath. While it is still fluid in the gap, it can be stretched, which permits higher drawing and better molecular orientation.

The method is used for high-performance fibres such as **para-aramid** fibres, including Kevlar, spun from concentrated sulphuric acid, and **lyocell**, spun from N-methylmorpholine-N-oxide (**NMMO**).

### Gel spinning
Gel spinning is a solution-based method used for **ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE)** fibres, such as Dyneema and Spectra. A dilute polymer solution is extruded and cooled to form a **gel filament**. This filament is then drawn to very high ratios, giving fibres with very high strength and **modulus** (resistance to stretching).

## The solution routes at a glance

| Route | How the filament solidifies | Example fibres |
|---|---|---|
| Dry spinning | Solvent evaporates in hot air or inert gas | Acetate, triacetate, elastane, some acrylic |
| Wet spinning | Coagulation in a bath | Viscose, acrylic, modacrylic |
| Dry-jet wet spinning | Air gap, then coagulation bath | Para-aramid, lyocell |
| Gel spinning | Cooled to a gel, then highly drawn | UHMWPE |

## Key takeaways
- Solution spinning is used when a polymer cannot be melted without decomposing; the polymer is dissolved to form a dope.
- Dry spinning evaporates a volatile solvent in a heated chamber; the vapour is recovered for reuse.
- Wet spinning solidifies filaments in a coagulation bath by solvent exchange or chemical reaction, as in viscose regeneration.
- Wet spinning is the slowest principal route, and dry spinning is slower than melt spinning.
- Dry-jet wet spinning and gel spinning allow high drawing, giving high orientation or very high strength.
