# Comparing Routes and Fibre Properties
You have now met the three principal spinning routes and two specialised variants. This final lesson puts the routes side by side, shows how each one leaves its mark on the finished fibre, and gives you a simple way to reason about which route a polymer needs.

## The three principal routes side by side
Melt, dry and wet spinning differ in five ways: the state of the polymer, how the filament solidifies, processing speed, cost and the need for solvent. Together, these differences decide which method suits a particular polymer and end use.

| Feature | Melt spinning | Dry spinning | Wet spinning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polymer state | Melt | Solution | Solution |
| Solidification | Cooling | Solvent evaporation | Coagulation in a bath |
| Solvent recovery | None | Required | Required |
| Relative speed | Highest | Intermediate | Lowest |
| Relative cost | Lowest | Higher | Higher |
| Examples | Polyester, nylon, polypropylene | Acetate, triacetate, elastane, some acrylic | Viscose, acrylic, modacrylic |

### Why the speeds differ
The speed of each route is set by how quickly the filament can become solid:

- In **melt spinning**, the filament only has to cool, which a controlled air flow does quickly.
- In **dry spinning**, solvent has to evaporate, and the evaporation rate controls solidification.
- In **wet spinning**, coagulation depends on chemical and diffusion processes, which are the slowest of the three.

### Why the costs differ
Melt spinning needs no solvent, so it avoids solvent recovery altogether. Dry spinning must recover the solvent vapour and needs safety and environmental controls. Wet spinning must handle large volumes of liquid for coagulation, washing and chemical recovery.

## How the route shapes the fibre
The spinning route affects the structure of the fibre, not just the production conditions. The final properties come from the interaction of four things: **extrusion**, **solidification**, **spinning speed** and **drawing**.

### Cross-section
In melt spinning, the **spinneret hole** is the main control over filament cross-section, so round, trilobal or hollow holes give round, trilobal or hollow filaments. In solution spinning, the **solidification mechanism** also influences the final shape. That is why dry-spun acetate can be lobed, dry-spun acrylic dog-bone shaped and wet-spun viscose serrated.

### Internal structure
Wet-spun fibres such as viscose may develop a **skin–core structure** during coagulation and regeneration: the outer layer, which solidifies first, differs in structure from the inside. This influences both strength and dyeing behaviour.

### Void content
Some solution-spun fibres can contain **voids**, tiny empty spaces inside the fibre. Voids influence the fibre's **lustre** and its **dye uptake**.

### Orientation and crystallinity
**Molecular orientation** describes how well the polymer molecules line up along the fibre axis. **Crystallinity** describes how much of the polymer is packed into closely ordered regions. Across all three methods, both are influenced by spinning speed and by the extent of drawing after extrusion. This is the same principle you saw with LOY, POY and FDY in melt spinning.

> **Key idea:** Polymer behaviour decides the spinning route. The solidification mechanism and drawing conditions then shape the filament's structure and properties.

## Choosing a route: a simple decision path
You can reason through the choice in a few steps:

1. **Can the polymer be melted without decomposing?** If yes, melt spinning is preferred: it is fast, comparatively simple and needs no solvent.
2. **If not, dissolve it to make a dope.** Solution spinning is now required.
3. **Is the solvent volatile enough to evaporate in hot gas?** Then dry spinning can be used.
4. **Does the filament solidify by solvent exchange or chemical reaction in a bath?** Then wet spinning is the route, as with viscose.
5. **Does the fibre need high molecular orientation or very high strength?** Dry-jet wet spinning (para-aramid, lyocell) or gel spinning (UHMWPE) extends solution spinning for these demands.

### Applying the path
- **Polyester and nylon** melt without decomposing, so they are melt spun.
- **Cellulose** cannot be melted, so it is solution spun: as viscose through wet spinning, or as lyocell through dry-jet wet spinning.
- **Acrylic** is also solution spun, by dry spinning or by wet spinning in solvents such as DMF or DMAc.

## Key takeaways
- Melt, dry and wet spinning differ in polymer state, solidification, speed, cost and solvent recovery.
- Melt spinning is the fastest and cheapest; wet spinning is the slowest and needs the most liquid handling.
- In melt spinning the spinneret hole sets the cross-section; in solution spinning, solidification also shapes it.
- Wet-spun fibres can have a skin–core structure, and some solution-spun fibres contain voids.
- Spinning speed and drawing control orientation and crystallinity, whichever route is used.
