# From Polymer to Filament: Melt Spinning
Polyester, nylon and polypropylene do not grow as fibres; they start as solid polymer. In this lesson you will see how any man-made fibre is formed, why there are two main routes, and how the fastest and cheapest of them, melt spinning, works step by step.

## What "spinning" means for man-made fibres
Man-made fibres are produced by forcing a liquid polymer through the fine holes of a **spinneret**, a plate pierced with many tiny holes, and then solidifying the emerging streams into **filaments**. This process is called **spinning** or **extrusion**.

It is not the same as spinning in yarn manufacture, where short staple fibres are twisted together. Here, "spinning" means creating the fibre itself.

Before it can be extruded, the polymer must be turned into a liquid or fluid state. There are two principal routes:

- **Melt spinning:** the polymer is melted.
- **Solution spinning:** the polymer is dissolved in a solvent. This route is further divided into **dry spinning** and **wet spinning**, covered in the next lesson.

The route chosen depends mainly on the **thermal behaviour** of the polymer: what happens to it when it is heated.

> **Key idea:** The first question is always whether the polymer can be melted without decomposing. If it can, melt spinning is the natural choice.

## Which polymers are melt spun?
Melt spinning is used for polymers that can be melted without decomposition. Important examples are **polyester (PET)**, **nylon 6**, **nylon 6,6** and **polypropylene**.

In outline, the process converts polymer chips into a uniform melt, meters the melt through a spinneret, cools the extruded filaments and, where required, draws them to develop the right molecular orientation.

## The melt spinning sequence
1. **Drying and melting.** Polymer chips are dried and fed into a screw extruder, where they melt. Drying is particularly important for polyester, because moisture can cause **hydrolysis**, a reaction with water that breaks the polymer chains and reduces molecular weight.
2. **Metering and filtration.** A **gear pump** delivers a controlled, constant flow of melt, so each filament receives the same amount of polymer. The melt then passes through a **filter pack**, which removes gels and particles that could block the spinneret or weaken the filaments.
3. **Extrusion.** The melt is forced through the spinneret. The number of holes sets the number of filaments, and the shape of the holes sets the filament cross-section. Common shapes are round, trilobal and hollow.
4. **Quenching.** The hot filaments are cooled and solidified by a controlled flow of air in a **quench chamber**. Cooling must be uniform, or the filaments will not have consistent properties.
5. **Finish application and take-up.** A **spin finish** is applied to control friction and static. The filaments are then guided over **godet rollers** and wound into packages.
6. **Drawing.** The as-spun filaments may be stretched to several times their original length. Drawing can be a separate operation or integrated with spinning.

## Why drawing matters
When filaments first solidify, their long polymer molecules are only partly lined up. **Drawing** pulls them into closer alignment along the fibre axis, which is called increasing the **molecular orientation**.

The effect on properties is predictable: drawing **increases tenacity** (strength relative to fineness) and **reduces elongation** (how far the fibre stretches before breaking). A well-drawn filament is stronger but less stretchy than an undrawn one.

## Spinning speed and yarn forms
The **take-up speed**, the speed at which filaments are wound up, also influences the molecular orientation of the as-spun yarn. That in turn decides what processing the yarn still needs. Three common yarn forms result:

| Yarn form | How it is made | What it needs next |
|---|---|---|
| Low-oriented yarn (LOY) | Spun at lower speeds | Full drawing |
| Partially oriented yarn (POY) | Spun at higher speeds | Commonly used as feed yarn for draw-texturing |
| Fully drawn yarn (FDY) | Spinning and drawing in one continuous process | Drawing already complete |

**Draw-texturing** is a later process that draws the yarn and gives it bulk and texture at the same time, which is why partly oriented POY suits it well.

## Strengths and limitation of melt spinning
- **No solvent** is needed, so there is no solvent recovery in the spinning process.
- It is the **fastest and lowest-cost** route among the three principal methods: melt, dry and wet spinning.
- Its main **limitation** is that it works only with polymers that remain thermally stable at their melting temperature.

Polymers that decompose before they melt need a different approach, and that is the subject of the next lesson.

## Key takeaways
- Man-made fibres are formed by extruding a liquid polymer through a spinneret and solidifying the filaments.
- The route depends on the polymer's thermal behaviour: melt it if it can be melted without decomposing; otherwise dissolve it.
- Melt spinning runs through drying and melting, metering and filtration, extrusion, quenching, finish and take-up, and drawing.
- Drawing aligns molecules, increasing tenacity and reducing elongation; take-up speed gives LOY, POY or FDY.
- Melt spinning is the fastest, lowest-cost route and needs no solvent, but suits only thermally stable polymers.
