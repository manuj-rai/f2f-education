# What Makes a Favourite Garment
If one in six garments is never worn, the obvious next question is what makes the others earn their place. This lesson looks at why Dutch consumers name a garment as a favourite, why they let garments go, and what that suggests about comfort and sustainability.

## The reasons behind favourites
In the National Wardrobe Audit 2025, people were asked why a particular garment was a favourite. One answer stood out: **comfort and good fit** accounted for 43.3 per cent of responses, more than twice the next reason.

| Reason for a favourite garment | Share of responses |
|---|---|
| Comfort and good fit | 43.3% |
| Looks and style | 16.6% |
| Versatility | 15.4% |
| Material and quality | 6.8% |
| Personal, unique or brand value | 6.4% |
| Emotional or sentimental value | 3.2% |
| Occasion suitability | 3.1% |
| Sustainability | 1.3% |

Three points are worth noticing:
- **Comfort and fit dominate.** How a garment feels on the body matters more than anything else.
- **Versatility ranks third.** A garment that works in many situations gets more chances to be worn.
- **Sustainability ranks last**, at 1.3 per cent.

## Do consumers not care about sustainability?
The article is careful on this point. The finding does not show that Dutch consumers are indifferent to sustainability. It shows how they experience clothing day to day: through comfort, fit, appearance, versatility and use.

That is a more useful insight for a business. People may value sustainability in principle, but it is rarely the reason they reach for the same jacket every morning. If a brand wants its sustainable products to be used, it has to deliver the qualities people notice when they wear them.

> **Key idea:** Consumers may value sustainability, but they experience a garment through comfort, fit, looks and versatility. Sustainability works best when it is built into those qualities.

## Could comfort be a hidden driver of sustainability?
The article proposes a possible chain linking comfort to longer garment life:

1. **Comfort** makes the garment pleasant to wear.
2. **Attachment** grows as the wearer comes to rely on it.
3. **Repeated use** follows, across more days and occasions.
4. **Longer retention** keeps it in the wardrobe and in use.
5. **Potentially fewer replacements** are needed.

Treat this as a **hypothesis**: a proposed relationship that still has to be tested. The author states that it would be premature to claim comfort automatically creates longer garment life. But the link is plausible and worth researching, because it would let brands pursue sustainability through qualities consumers already value.

It also reframes responsible consumption. Rather than asking only why people buy, the industry may need to understand why they keep choosing the same garment.

## Why garments leave the wardrobe
The audit also looked at the other end of the journey. Among discarded garments, the leading reasons were:

| Reason for discarding | Share |
|---|---|
| No longer fits | 31% |
| Worn out | 28% |
| No longer liked | 21% |

Each reason points to a different lever:
- **Fit** appears at both ends of a garment’s life. It is the biggest reason for loving a garment and the most common reason for letting one go.
- **Wear-out** points to material choice, construction and testing, which decide physical durability.
- **No longer liked** points to style and attachment: designs people keep wanting, rather than ones made only for novelty.

## Comfort as a product strategy
If comfort and fit drive repeated wear, they cannot be left to chance or treated as a marketing word. The article describes comfort as a product strategy covering fit, movement, climate, construction, finishing and care.

Each of these is decided by designers, pattern makers, fabric developers and suppliers, long before the garment reaches a shop. A soft fabric with a poor fit, or a good fit in a fabric that is hard to care for, will not deliver the comfort that keeps a garment in use.

### Questions to ask
- Are we designing for actual wear, or only for the moment of sale?
- What makes some of our products go dormant in customers’ wardrobes?
- Can sustainability be built into the reasons people already love a garment?

## Key takeaways
- Comfort and good fit were the top reason for a favourite garment, at 43.3 per cent of responses.
- Only 1.3 per cent named sustainability, which reflects how people experience clothes, not a lack of concern.
- The chain from comfort to attachment, repeated use and retention is a promising hypothesis, not a proven fact.
- Garments are discarded mainly because they no longer fit (31%), are worn out (28%) or are no longer liked (21%).
- Treat comfort as a product strategy: fit, movement, climate, construction, finishing and care.
