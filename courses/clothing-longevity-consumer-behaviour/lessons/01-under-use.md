# The Hidden Problem of Under-Use
Fashion is usually measured by what people buy: sales, spending, market size and purchase frequency. This lesson moves the focus to what happens after the purchase, and explains why the garments that sit unworn in a wardrobe may matter as much as the ones that are bought.

## A garment’s life starts at the till
Sales figures end the story at the moment of purchase. For the wearer, that is where it begins. They decide whether the garment feels right, wear it for different occasions, keep or discard it, perhaps repair or resell it, and in doing so decide how long it stays in use.

We can picture this as a **wardrobe journey**:

1. Purchase
2. Ownership
3. First wear
4. Repeated wear
5. Repair, resale or reuse
6. Disposal

Most business data covers only the first step. The article argues that the biggest opportunity may sit between ownership and repeated wear: the point where a garment either becomes part of someone’s life or quietly drops out of use.

## Why the Netherlands is a useful case
The article uses the Netherlands to explore this question. The country combines a digitally mature consumer market, a large international apparel trade, an established fashion culture and an increasingly formal circular-textile policy. That mix makes it a good place to see how consumers, retailers and regulators interact.

The Netherlands has more than 18 million residents, but its role in fashion is much larger than its population suggests.

### Read trade figures with care
These numbers do not mean Dutch shoppers personally buy €17.9 billion of clothing. The Port of Rotterdam and the wider logistics network make the country a gateway into Europe. CBI, the Centre for the Promotion of Imports from developing countries, notes that the Netherlands **re-exports** around 90 per cent of the apparel value it imports: the goods arrive there and are then sold on to other European markets.

This is a useful habit for any analyst. An import figure describes trade flows, not necessarily local consumption.

## Inside the Dutch wardrobe
The best recent evidence on how Dutch people actually use clothing comes from the **National Wardrobe Audit 2025**, carried out by Hogeschool Rotterdam with partner institutions. Researchers inventoried 958 wardrobes belonging to people aged 15 to 70. The audit covered clothing only, leaving out socks, underwear and shoes.

The main findings:
- The average wardrobe held **125 garments**.
- Women owned 148 garments on average, men 102.
- Around 9 per cent of garments were second-hand.
- About **one in six garments had never been worn**, even when it suited the season.

> **Key idea:** The “clothing mountain” is not only about how much clothing enters the wardrobe. It is also about how much of that clothing ever becomes useful.

## Why under-use matters
**Under-use** means owning garments that are rarely or never worn. Every garment took materials, energy and labour to make and transport. If it is never worn, none of that effort delivers any value.

The article makes the point sharply: a sustainable garment that is never worn still represents a poor use of resources. Better fibres and cleaner processes matter, but they cannot make up for a product nobody uses.

### From voluntary claims to business systems
This question is becoming more pressing because the Netherlands has moved sustainability beyond voluntary brand messages. **Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)** for textiles became operational in 2023. EPR makes producers responsible for their products after use; for Dutch textiles it covers collection, reuse and recycling. The government has also set up a Circular Textile Programme for 2025–2030, part of a longer move towards a circular textile chain.

For a fashion company, the question shifts from “Will consumers prefer sustainable fashion?” to “Can we design, source, sell, collect, reuse and recycle our products within a circular system?” The article argues that **retention**, keeping garments in active use for longer, deserves a place in that conversation.

## Key takeaways
- A garment’s commercial life begins, rather than ends, at purchase.
- The Netherlands combines a digital consumer market, a European logistics hub and formal circular-textile policy.
- Most Dutch apparel imports pass through: around 90 per cent of their value is re-exported.
- The 2025 audit found 125 garments in the average wardrobe, with about one in six never worn.
- Under-use wastes resources, so garment retention belongs in circular-fashion strategy.
