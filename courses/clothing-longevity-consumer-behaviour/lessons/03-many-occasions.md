# One Consumer, Many Occasions
Most consumer research sorts people into groups by age, gender or income. This lesson explains why that is only part of the picture, and how the occasions a person dresses for shape what they want from each garment.

## The limits of demographic segmentation
**Demographic segmentation** groups consumers by characteristics such as age, gender, income or location. It is useful, but it treats each person as one consumer with one set of needs.

In reality, the same person may dress for many situations in a single week:
- work
- exercise
- travel
- time with friends
- nightlife
- dating
- formal occasions
- private life at home

The value of a garment changes with the situation. Trousers may be valued because they look professional at work. The same person’s sportswear may be valued for movement and comfort. An outfit for a date may be chosen because it builds confidence or communicates identity, while clothes worn privately may be picked mainly for comfort, fit or personal confidence.

This is **situational consumption**: studying clothing by the occasion it serves, not only by who buys it. As the article puts it, there can be several markets inside one consumer.

## Clothing as communication
Research on dress and social impression shows that clothing can communicate information about attractiveness, identity and social signals: the messages others read from how we dress. But the meaning an observer reads into an outfit is not necessarily what the wearer intended.

For a brand, the useful commercial question is therefore not “What clothes make someone attractive?” but “How does the purpose of the occasion change what the consumer wants from clothing?” A work outfit, a gym outfit and a date outfit may be bought by the same person for very different reasons.

## When sport becomes everyday wear
The line between sportswear and everyday clothing is increasingly fluid. Market research identifies active lifestyles and demand for comfort as important drivers of sportswear in the Netherlands.

A consumer may buy a technical jacket, sweatshirt, trainers or performance trousers for sport, then wear them for commuting, travel, shopping or socialising. The product moves along a path: **performance → comfort → lifestyle → identity**.

This crossover is often called **athleisure**: performance-style clothing worn for everyday life. Instead of asking “Is this sportswear or fashion?”, the article suggests a better question for product teams: **how many situations can this product serve?** That links straight back to the wardrobe audit, where versatility was the third most common reason for a favourite garment.

> **Key idea:** A garment that works across many situations gets more chances to be worn. Versatility is a design target, not a happy accident.

## Practical, but not predictable
The Dutch market is often associated with practical, functional and understated clothing. CBI describes demand for comfortable and versatile garments, simple designs, easy-care fabrics and layering, helped by the country’s temperate climate.

But practical does not mean uniform. The Netherlands is also home to internationally recognised experimental and independent voices, from designers such as Iris van Herpen and Viktor & Rolf to streetwear names including Daily Paper, Patta and Filling Pieces.

A better summary of the Dutch consumer is **functionality plus individuality**, rather than simple minimalism. People can want comfort and practicality while still using clothes to express identity, creativity, culture and lifestyle. A range that serves only one side of that balance risks missing the other.

## The missing wardrobe
Every study has limits, and the National Wardrobe Audit excluded underwear, socks and shoes. Outerwear and everyday garments are the visible part of consumption. **Intimate apparel** represents a different, more private relationship with clothing.

The article suggests a future study could ask whether people choose intimate garments mainly for comfort, fit, confidence, personal identity, partner perception, sensuality, novelty, durability or sustainability. The point is not to assume the answer. The private wardrobe may follow a different consumption logic from the public one, and that deserves research of its own.

### Questions to ask
- Are we segmenting customers only by demographics, or also by situation?
- Can our performance products become lifestyle products?
- How many occasions does each style in our range really serve?

## Key takeaways
- One person can represent several clothing markets, from work and sport to dating and private life.
- Situational consumption asks how the occasion changes what a consumer wants from a garment.
- Sportswear increasingly crosses into everyday wear, so ask how many situations a product can serve.
- Dutch style combines functionality with individuality, not just minimalism.
- The audit left out intimate apparel, a private wardrobe that may follow its own logic.
