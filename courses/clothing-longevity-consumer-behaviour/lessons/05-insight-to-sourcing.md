# From Wearer Insight to Sourcing
Consumer research is often treated as a marketing task. This final lesson shows how what people value when they wear clothes travels back into product development and sourcing, and what the Dutch case teaches suppliers, including those in India.

## Consumer behaviour becomes a sourcing decision
Each quality a consumer values sets off a chain of decisions they will never see, but will feel every time they put the garment on.

| If the consumer values | The business must think about |
|---|---|
| Comfort | Fabric hand-feel, stretch, construction, fit, supplier capability, quality control |
| Versatility | Multi-occasion design, product architecture, sourcing flexibility |
| Durability | Material selection, construction, testing, quality assurance |
| Unpredictable digital demand | Smaller first quantities, faster feedback, agile suppliers, replenishment |

**Hand-feel** is how a fabric feels to the touch, for example soft, crisp or smooth.

## The marketing × sourcing matrix
The article brings these links together in a matrix. Here is a selection of its rows:

| Consumer signal | Marketing response | Product response | Sourcing implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comfort | Communicate wear experience | Softness, movement, fit | Fabric and construction quality |
| Fit | Improve size communication | Accurate grading and fit | Supplier consistency |
| Versatility | Show multiple occasions | Multi-use silhouettes | Flexible development |
| Sustainability | Evidence-based communication | Durable or circular design | Traceability |
| Second-hand | Resale and take-back | Design for a second life | Reverse logistics |
| Digital demand | Test content quickly | Smaller initial batches | Agile production |
| Disposal | Repair and take-back | Repairable construction | Reverse logistics |

A few terms from the matrix: **grading** is scaling a pattern up and down to create each size; **traceability** is the ability to track a product and its materials through the supply chain; and **reverse logistics** is the flow of goods back from consumers for repair, resale, reuse or recycling.

> **Key idea:** Consumer research does not stop at marketing. It travels backwards into the supply chain.

## The Netherlands as a sourcing lesson for India
India is already part of the Dutch apparel supply chain. The main suppliers to the Netherlands by import value in 2024 included:

| Country | Share of import value |
|---|---|
| China | 19.9% |
| Germany | 16.1% |
| Bangladesh | 12.0% |
| Türkiye | 8.5% |
| Vietnam | 5.0% |
| Poland | 4.0% |
| India | 3.3% |

The article argues that India’s opportunity should not be reduced to producing at a lower price. The stronger offer combines quality, fit, functionality, reliability, compliance, traceability, design and delivery. Seen this way, the Netherlands is not just an export destination but a window on what mature European consumers, retailers and supply chains increasingly expect.

The lesson is not that India should become the Netherlands; the two differ in climate, culture, income, consumer structure and fashion tradition. It is to understand the consumer beyond the transaction. Five areas stand out:

1. **Design for repeated use.** A garment that works across several situations can create more practical value than one designed only for novelty.
2. **Treat comfort as a product strategy.** Fit, movement, climate, construction, finishing and care all count.
3. **Build circularity into the product lifecycle.** Plan repair, resale, take-back and traceability before disposal.
4. **Use digital behaviour as a sourcing signal.** Search, engagement, conversion, reviews and returns are demand intelligence.
5. **Segment by situation, not only demographics.** One consumer can have several clothing needs in a single week.

## Consumption as a sequence of decisions
The bigger insight from the Dutch case is that fashion consumption is not one behaviour but a sequence of questions the wearer answers:
- **Context:** where am I going?
- **Comfort:** how do I want to feel?
- **Communication:** what do I want this outfit to say about me?
- **Convenience:** how easily can I find and buy it?
- **Use:** will I actually wear it?
- **Circularity:** what happens when I no longer need it?

This view is more useful than dividing people into “sustainable” and “non-sustainable” consumers. It also points to open research questions: whether comfort and fit predict repeated use, whether novelty speeds up replacement, and whether **garment retention** could become a measurable part of circular-fashion strategy.

The article ends with a question for designers, marketers, sourcing teams, researchers and policymakers alike. If fashion wants people to buy less and use products longer, should it focus less on asking consumers to care about sustainability, and more on creating products they genuinely want to keep wearing?

## Key takeaways
- What consumers value when wearing clothes shapes fabric, construction, quality and supplier choices.
- The marketing × sourcing matrix links each consumer signal to product and sourcing responses.
- India supplied 3.3 per cent of Dutch apparel imports by value in 2024; its stronger offer goes beyond price.
- Five priorities: repeated use, comfort, built-in circularity, digital demand signals and situational segmentation.
- Garment retention could become a measurable goal of circular-fashion strategy.
