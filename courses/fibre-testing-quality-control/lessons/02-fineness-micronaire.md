# Fineness and the Micronaire Value
Fineness is one of the properties that most strongly limits how fine a yarn a fibre can make. In this lesson you will meet the units used for fibre fineness, five ways of measuring it, and the reason a micronaire reading should never be read on its own.

## What fineness means
**Fineness** describes how thick a fibre is. It is expressed in one of two ways: as **linear density**, which is mass per unit length, or as **diameter**. Cotton also has its own measure, the micronaire value.

| Measure | Unit | Mainly used for |
|---|---|---|
| Linear density | tex, decitex (dtex), millitex (mtex) | Man-made fibres often use dtex or denier |
| Diameter | Micrometres (µm) | Wool |
| Micronaire value | Micrograms per inch (µg/in) | Cotton |

One tex is a mass of 1 gram per 1,000 metres. Fibres are so fine that smaller units give more convenient numbers: one decitex is 1 gram per 10,000 metres, and one millitex is 1 milligram per 1,000 metres. Denier, the mass in grams of 9,000 metres, is also widely used for man-made fibres.

The micronaire value is a special case. It reflects **both fibre fineness and maturity**, which is why it needs careful interpretation.

## Five ways to measure fineness

### Airflow method (micronaire)
A cotton sample of fixed weight is compressed in a chamber of fixed volume. Air is passed through the plug of fibres, and the resistance to airflow is measured and converted to a micronaire value.

Why does this work? Finer fibres have more surface area for the same weight, so a plug made of them resists the air more strongly and gives a lower reading. Thin-walled, immature fibres behave in a similar way. A low micronaire value may therefore mean fine fibres, immature fibres, or a combination of both.

> **Key idea:** A low micronaire reading does not prove that a cotton is fine. It may be immature, so always interpret micronaire together with a maturity measure.

### Gravimetric method
A bundle of fibres is cut to a known length, the fibres are counted, and the bundle is weighed. The total length is the number of fibres multiplied by the cut length, and linear density is calculated from the weight and total length.

For example, suppose 100 fibres are cut to 10 mm each, giving 1 metre of fibre in total, and the bundle weighs 0.15 mg. That is 0.15 mg per metre, or 150 mg per 1,000 metres, so the linear density is 0.15 tex, which is 1.5 dtex.

### Microscopic method
Fibre diameters are measured under a microscope, usually a **projection microscope**, which projects an enlarged image of the fibre onto a screen for measurement. This is a standard reference method for wool.

### Vibroscope method
A single fibre is held under tension and made to vibrate. Its linear density is calculated from its natural vibration frequency, in the same way that the note of a guitar string depends on its tension and its mass. This method is used for man-made fibres.

### Optical instruments
Laser and image-based systems measure wool fibre diameter quickly, on large numbers of fibres. They are useful where many results are needed in a short time.

## Why fineness matters
Finer fibres allow more fibres to fit into a given yarn cross-section. With more fibres sharing the load and the random variation in fibre numbers mattering less, this supports:

- finer yarn counts
- better yarn strength and evenness, within suitable limits
- a softer handle

Fineness is therefore an important factor in setting the **spinning limit**, the finest yarn that a fibre can practically be spun into. It is also one reason why maturity and fineness must be considered together: a cotton that looks fine on micronaire but is actually immature will not deliver the benefits of genuinely fine fibre.

## Key takeaways
- Fineness is expressed as linear density (tex, dtex, mtex or denier), diameter in µm, or the micronaire value for cotton.
- The airflow method gives micronaire, which reflects both fineness and maturity.
- A low micronaire may mean fine fibres, immature fibres or both, so read it with a maturity result.
- The gravimetric, microscopic, vibroscope and optical methods suit different fibres; the projection microscope is a reference method for wool.
- Finer fibres put more fibres into the yarn cross-section, supporting finer counts, a softer handle and, within limits, better strength and evenness.
