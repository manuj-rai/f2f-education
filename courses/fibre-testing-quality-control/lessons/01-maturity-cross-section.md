# Fibre Structure: Maturity and Cross-Section
Fibre is the basic raw material for every yarn and fabric. Its properties limit how well it can be spun and how good the yarn and fabric can be. In this first lesson you will see why mills test fibre before processing it, then look at two properties that describe a fibre's structure: maturity and cross-section.

## Why test fibre before spinning?
Fibre testing is a key part of **raw-material quality control**. The results guide decisions long before any yarn is made. Mills use them to:

- select bales and plan how they are mixed
- set machine and processing conditions
- predict the quality of the yarn and fabric
- assess and value cotton for commercial purposes

In this course we work through the main properties a testing laboratory checks. We start with structure, then move on to fineness, strength, length and moisture, and finish with trash, neps and colour. Most examples are for cotton, with wool and man-made fibres included where their tests differ.

## Maturity: how well developed is the fibre wall?
A cotton fibre first grows as a thin tube. Cellulose is then deposited on the inside of that tube, building up a thicker **secondary wall**. The hollow canal left in the middle is called the **lumen**.

**Maturity** is the degree to which this wall has developed. It mainly applies to cotton and other natural cellulosic fibres, because it describes how a plant fibre grew. Fibres fall into three broad classes:

| Fibre class | Wall development |
|---|---|
| Mature (normal) | Thick secondary wall |
| Immature (thin-walled) | Thin wall |
| Dead | Little or no secondary wall |

### How maturity is expressed
- **Percentage of mature fibres** in the sample.
- **Maturity ratio (M)**, which sums up maturity as a single value.
- **Immature fibre content (IFC)**, the percentage of immature fibres in the sample.

In the caustic soda method, the maturity ratio is calculated as **M = (N − D) ÷ 200 + 0.70**, where N is the percentage of normal (mature) fibres and D is the percentage of dead fibres.

For example, if a sample had 70 per cent normal fibres and 10 per cent dead fibres, M = (70 − 10) ÷ 200 + 0.70 = 0.30 + 0.70 = 1.00. A higher share of dead fibres would pull the ratio down.

### How maturity is tested
1. **Caustic soda swelling.** Fibres are treated with about 18 per cent sodium hydroxide and examined under a microscope. Swelling makes the relationship between wall thickness and lumen width easy to see, so each fibre can be classed as normal, thin-walled or dead.
2. **Polarised light.** Fibres are viewed between crossed polarising filters. Differences in their interference colours help separate mature from immature fibres.
3. **Differential dyeing.** The sample is dyed with red and green dyes. Mature fibres take up more red dye, while immature fibres look greener, which gives a quick visual indication of maturity.
4. **Instruments.** The AFIS (Advanced Fibre Information System) can report maturity ratio and immature fibre content. The micronaire value, covered in the next lesson, is also influenced by maturity.

### Why maturity matters
Immature fibres break more easily and are more prone to forming **neps**, small knots of tangled fibres. They can produce more waste and more yarn breaks. They also absorb dye differently from mature fibres, which affects dyeing quality.

> **Key idea:** Immature fibres cause trouble at several stages: breakage, neps and waste during processing, then uneven colour after dyeing.

## Cross-section: the shape of a fibre
**Cross-section** is the shape and structure of a fibre seen in a plane perpendicular to its length, as if you had sliced straight through it. Each fibre type has a typical shape:

| Fibre | Typical cross-section |
|---|---|
| Cotton | Bean or kidney shape, with a collapsed lumen |
| Wool | Roughly circular to oval, with a scaly surface |
| Silk | Roughly triangular, with rounded corners |
| Viscose | Irregular, with a serrated outline |
| Polyester and nylon | Usually circular; can be modified, for example trilobal |

### How cross-section is examined
- **Microtome sectioning.** A bundle of fibres is set in a supporting medium and cut into thin slices, often with a hand microtome such as the Hardy microtome. The slices are then viewed under a microscope.
- **Scanning electron microscopy (SEM).** This gives detailed images of the fibre surface and cross-section.
- **Image analysis.** Software measures area, perimeter and wall thickness from digital images of cross-sections. These measures can also be used to assess maturity.

### Why cross-section matters
Cross-section can affect **lustre, handle, stiffness and cover**. A trilobal man-made fibre, for example, reflects light in a way that produces a silk-like sheen. The shape also helps to identify an unknown fibre.

Surface features, such as the scales on wool, affect handle and friction too. They should be considered separately from the cross-sectional shape.

## Key takeaways
- Fibre testing guides bale selection and mixing, machine settings, yarn quality prediction and cotton valuation.
- Maturity is the development of the cotton fibre's secondary wall; mature, immature and dead fibres differ in wall thickness.
- Maturity is expressed as percentage of mature fibres, maturity ratio or IFC, and tested by caustic soda swelling, polarised light, differential dyeing or AFIS.
- Immature fibres lead to more breakage, neps, waste and uneven dyeing.
- Cross-section affects lustre, handle, stiffness and cover, and is examined by microtome, SEM and image analysis.
