# Fibre Strength and Elongation
How much force a fibre can take, and how far it stretches before it breaks, shape both yarn quality and how fast the fibre can be processed. This lesson covers the measures and instruments used for each property, and why a strength result is incomplete without its gauge length.

## How fibre strength is expressed
**Fibre strength** is the force required to break a fibre or a bundle of fibres. Three measures are in common use:

| Measure | What it is | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Breaking force | The force at which the fibre breaks | Centinewtons (cN) or grams-force |
| Tenacity | Breaking force divided by linear density | g/tex or cN/tex |
| Pressley Index | Bundle breaking load divided by bundle weight | Pounds per milligram |

Breaking force on its own favours coarse fibres, which need more force to break simply because they contain more material. **Tenacity** removes that effect by dividing by linear density, so fibres of different fineness can be compared fairly.

For example, a fibre of 0.15 tex that breaks at 4.5 cN has a tenacity of 4.5 ÷ 0.15 = 30 cN/tex.

## Bundles or single fibres?

### Bundle testing
Cotton fibres are usually tested as **flat bundles**, because single cotton fibres are short and variable. Breaking many fibres together gives a more representative result. Three bundle testers are widely used:

- **Pressley tester:** breaks the bundle at zero gauge length, with the jaws touching.
- **Stelometer:** breaks the bundle at a 1/8-inch (3.2 mm) gauge length and also records elongation.
- **High Volume Instrument (HVI):** tests bundles automatically at 1/8-inch gauge and reports strength in g/tex.

### Single fibre testing
Man-made fibres and long natural fibres can be tested one at a time. This is done on tensile testers that work on the **constant rate of extension (CRE)** principle, where the fibre is stretched at a steady rate while the force is recorded.

## Why gauge length matters
**Gauge length** is the distance between the two jaws that hold the specimen. It affects the measured strength.

A fibre tends to break at its weakest point. At zero gauge length, the jaws touch, so weak sections along the fibre have less influence on the result. At a 1/8-inch gauge length, a longer portion of each fibre is tested, so a weak place is more likely to fall within it.

> **Key idea:** A strength figure is only meaningful with its gauge length. Before comparing two results, check that both were measured at the same gauge.

### Why strength matters
Fibre strength contributes to yarn strength. It also influences processing performance, including the speeds a mill can safely run and how often ends break.

## Elongation: how far a fibre stretches
**Elongation** is the percentage increase in fibre length at break, relative to its original length:

**Elongation (%) = (extension at break ÷ initial length) × 100**

For example, a fibre tested at an initial length of 10 mm that extends by 0.8 mm before breaking has an elongation of (0.8 ÷ 10) × 100 = 8 per cent.

### How elongation is measured
Elongation is recorded during the strength test itself, using instruments such as the Stelometer, the HVI or a single-fibre tensile tester. One break therefore gives both results.

The tester also plots a **load–elongation curve**, showing how load builds up as the fibre stretches. The area under this curve gives the **work of rupture**, the energy needed to break the fibre, which reflects its **toughness**.

### Typical differences between fibres

| Fibre group | Examples | Elongation |
|---|---|---|
| Natural cellulosic | Cotton, linen | Low |
| Animal | Wool | Much higher |
| Man-made | Nylon, polyester | Much higher |

### Why elongation matters
Adequate elongation lets fibres absorb sudden loads during processing with less breakage. Two fibres may break at the same force, but the one that stretches further can soak up more of the sudden jerks that occur on machines. Fibre elongation also contributes to yarn elongation and to the durability of the finished fabric.

## Key takeaways
- Fibre strength is expressed as breaking force, tenacity (g/tex or cN/tex) or the Pressley Index.
- Tenacity divides breaking force by linear density, so fibres of different fineness can be compared fairly.
- Cotton is tested in flat bundles on the Pressley tester (zero gauge), Stelometer or HVI (both 1/8 inch); man-made fibres can be tested singly on CRE testers.
- Always state the gauge length, because it changes the measured strength.
- Elongation is recorded in the same test; the load–elongation curve gives the work of rupture, a measure of toughness.
