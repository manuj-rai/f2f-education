# Moisture Regain and Other Key Checks
Fibres take up water from the air, and because fibre is bought and sold by weight, that water has a price. This final lesson covers moisture regain, then the other routine checks for trash, neps, colour and stickiness, and ends by showing how all the results fit together.

## Moisture regain and moisture content
There are two ways to express the water in a fibre sample. W is the weight of the sample and D is its oven-dry weight.

- **Moisture regain** is the weight of water as a percentage of the oven-dry weight: **(W − D) ÷ D × 100**
- **Moisture content** is the weight of water as a percentage of the total sample weight: **(W − D) ÷ W × 100**

For example, a sample weighing 108.5 g that weighs 100 g after oven-drying holds 8.5 g of water. Its moisture regain is 8.5 per cent, but its moisture content is 8.5 ÷ 108.5 × 100, or about 7.8 per cent. Regain is the larger figure because it divides by the smaller, dry weight.

### Standard testing conditions
Moisture regain depends on the surrounding atmosphere, so fibres are **conditioned** (left to settle in a set atmosphere) and tested under specified conditions. A common standard atmosphere is 20 °C and 65 per cent relative humidity; some tropical testing conditions use 27 °C. Approximate values at standard conditions are:

| Fibre | Approximate regain (%) |
|---|---|
| Wool | 16 |
| Viscose | 13 |
| Silk | 11 |
| Cotton | 8.5 |
| Nylon | 4 |
| Acrylic | 1.5 |
| Polyester | 0.4 |

Commercial regain values vary with fibre form and the applicable standard, so check the relevant standard before using any figure in a trade calculation.

### How moisture is measured
- **Oven-drying:** the sample is weighed, dried at about 105–110 °C until its weight is constant, and weighed again. This is the reference method.
- **Electrical meters:** they measure electrical resistance or capacitance, which change with moisture, and give quick readings on bales and packages.
- **Infrared methods:** they measure how much infrared radiation the water in the sample absorbs.

### Why moisture matters
Invoice weight may be based on a specified **commercial regain**:

**Commercial weight = oven-dry weight × (100 + commercial regain) ÷ 100**

So, with an illustrative commercial regain of 10 per cent, 500 kg of oven-dry fibre would be invoiced as 550 kg. Moisture also affects:

- **Processing:** very dry fibres build static charge; very damp fibres may stick and cause lapping, where fibres wrap around rollers.
- **Strength:** cotton generally becomes stronger when wet, while viscose generally becomes weaker.
- **Comfort:** fibres with higher regain can absorb more moisture from the body.
- **Dimensions:** moisture makes fibres swell, which can influence how a fabric holds its size.

## Other routine checks
- **Trash** is non-fibrous matter in cotton, such as leaf, seed-coat fragments, stalk and dust. The Shirley Trash Analyser measures it as a percentage by weight, while HVI estimates it from camera images. High trash increases waste and can cause machinery, yarn and fabric faults.
- **Neps** are small knots of tangled fibres, which can form from immature fibres during ginning and processing. AFIS reports nep count per gram, and nep boards can also be used. Neps may show as visible specks in yarn and fabric, especially after dyeing.
- **Colour** of cotton is measured by HVI. Reflectance (**Rd**) indicates brightness, and yellowness (**+b**) is used with Rd to set the colour grade. Colour affects cotton value and dyeing or bleaching behaviour.
- **Stickiness** comes from sugars produced by insects (honeydew) or the plant. Instruments assess sticky points by heating or pressing the fibre. Sticky cotton clings to rollers and card clothing and interrupts production.
- **Man-made fibre checks** include heat shrinkage, which affects stability in dyeing and finishing, and spin-finish content, which influences friction and static.

## Putting the results together
Fibre properties are interrelated, so each result should be read alongside the others:

- Micronaire reflects both fineness and maturity, so interpret the two together.
- Immature fibres can contribute to weakness and neps.
- Fineness and length together set the practical spinning limit.
- Short fibre content affects yarn evenness and hairiness.
- Moisture affects strength, static and processing behaviour.

> **Key idea:** No single test is sufficient to judge fibre quality. Mills use the combined results to select and mix fibres, set processing conditions and keep yarn quality consistent.

## Key takeaways
- Moisture regain is water as a percentage of oven-dry weight; moisture content uses total weight.
- Test under standard conditions, such as 20 °C and 65 per cent relative humidity; oven-drying is the reference method.
- Commercial weight is based on oven-dry weight plus a specified commercial regain.
- Trash, neps, colour and stickiness are routine cotton checks; heat shrinkage and spin finish matter for man-made fibres.
- Judge fibre quality on the full set of results, never on one number.
