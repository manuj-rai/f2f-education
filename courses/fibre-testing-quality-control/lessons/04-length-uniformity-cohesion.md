# Length, Uniformity and Cohesion
Fibre length is a major factor in spinning performance, but an average length is not enough on its own. You also need to know how evenly the lengths are spread and how well the fibres hold together before they are twisted. This lesson covers all three.

## Why fibre length matters
Fibre length affects the yarn **count range**, **strength**, **evenness** and **hairiness**. Longer fibres generally give better fibre control and can contribute to stronger, smoother yarns.

Length also decides machine settings. In **drafting**, a sliver or roving is drawn out to make it thinner by passing it between pairs of rollers, each pair running faster than the one before. The distance between the roller pairs is chosen according to fibre length, so that the fibres are controlled as they move.

## How length is expressed
Fibre length can be given as a staple length, judged by hand stapling, or by several instrument measures:

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Upper Half Mean Length (UHML) | Mean length of the longer half of the fibres by weight; related to staple length |
| Mean Length (ML) | Average length of all the fibres |
| 2.5% span length | Length exceeded by 2.5% of fibres in a fibrogram; a common measure of effective length |
| 50% span length | Length exceeded by 50% of fibres in the same test |

A **fibrogram** is the length diagram produced by an instrument called the fibrograph. Length is measured by hand stapling, the Baer sorter, the fibrograph, HVI and AFIS.

## Length uniformity
In fibre testing, **uniformity** usually means the distribution of fibre lengths within a sample. The term can also describe consistency in other properties, such as fineness or maturity. Three measures are used for length uniformity.

### Uniformity Index (UI)
Reported by HVI: **UI = (ML ÷ UHML) × 100**

If every fibre were the same length, the mean length would equal the upper half mean length and UI would be 100. The more short fibres a sample contains, the further the mean falls below the UHML. For example, a cotton with a mean length of 24 mm and a UHML of 30 mm has a UI of (24 ÷ 30) × 100 = 80.

### Uniformity Ratio (UR)
Reported by the fibrograph: **UR = (50% span length ÷ 2.5% span length) × 100**

### Short Fibre Content (SFC)
The percentage of fibres shorter than **12.7 mm (0.5 inch)**.

Higher UI or UR and lower SFC all indicate greater length uniformity. Length and uniformity are measured with the fibrograph, HVI and AFIS. Older reference methods, such as the **Baer sorter** and **comb sorter**, arrange fibres by length to produce a length distribution diagram.

### Why uniformity matters
Uniform fibres draft more smoothly and contribute to an even yarn. Short fibres are harder for the drafting rollers to control, so a high short fibre content can increase **thick and thin places**, **hairiness**, **fly** (loose fibres released into the air) and **waste**.

> **Key idea:** Two cottons with the same UHML can spin very differently. The one with more short fibre is likely to give a less even, hairier yarn and more waste.

## Cohesiveness: fibres that cling together
**Cohesiveness** is the ability of fibres to cling together during processing. It holds the fibre web, sliver and roving together before the yarn is given its twist. Cohesion is influenced by fibre surface, shape and crimp:

- **Cotton:** natural convolutions, the twists that occur along the fibre.
- **Wool:** surface scales and natural crimp.
- **Man-made fibres:** crimp added during manufacture, plus a **spin finish** that controls fibre-to-fibre friction.

**Crimp** is the waviness of a fibre. It is important for wool and man-made staple fibres, and it affects cohesion, bulk, warmth and handle.

### How cohesion is tested
1. **Fibre-to-fibre friction test.** A fibre or group of fibres is pulled against another and the frictional force is measured. The coefficient of friction is calculated from the result.
2. **Sliver cohesion test.** A sliver or roving is drafted between pairs of rollers, and the force needed is measured as the drafting or cohesive force.
3. **Crimp measurement.** For man-made fibres, the number of crimps per unit length and the crimp percentage are measured, since crimp strongly affects cohesion.

### Finding the balance
Cohesion is a case where more is not always better. Too little causes the web or sliver to break and creates fibre fly. Too much makes drafting difficult and can produce an uneven sliver. Smooth processing needs a suitable balance between the two.

## Key takeaways
- Fibre length affects count range, yarn strength, evenness and hairiness, and drafting settings are chosen to suit it.
- Length is expressed as staple length, UHML, mean length or span length.
- UI = (ML ÷ UHML) × 100 and UR = (50% ÷ 2.5% span length) × 100; SFC is the share of fibres under 12.7 mm.
- Higher UI or UR and lower SFC mean more uniform fibre, smoother drafting and a more even yarn.
- Cohesion comes from fibre surface, shape and crimp; too little or too much both cause processing problems.
